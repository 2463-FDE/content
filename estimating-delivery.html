Estimating Delivery, Not Effort

The blocker card teaches you to put two numbers on something that is stuck. This reading is the other half: how to size a whole feature with Claude, turn that into a date you hand a client, and carry enough buffer that hitting it doesn't cost you your evenings. Every claim here traces to a source listed at the bottom.

Start here

Your first number is wrong in a direction you can predict

This is not a character flaw and it does not improve with seniority. It is the most consistently measured finding in estimation research, so you may as well plan around it.

What's measured
The finding
What it means for you
How teams estimate
Expert judgement is the dominant strategy in practice, estimates are typically over-optimistic, and there is strong over-confidence in their accuracy. Mean effort overrun is about 30%, and it is not decreasing over time.S1
Assume roughly a third more than your gut, before you know anything else.
Your own confidence
Asked for dates they were 50%, 75% and 99% sure of, people finished by those dates 13%, 19% and 45% of the time.S2
"I'm sure" is worth about 45%. Your certainty is not evidence.
Somebody else's estimate of your work
The same bias reverses for outside observers — they are pessimistic about other people's tasks.S2
This is the seat Claude sits in. Its number usually runs high, and for a structural reason rather than a random one.
What AI does to the duration
Isolated greenfield task: 55.8% faster.S3 Complex enterprise task: about 21% faster.S4 Experienced maintainers on large mature repos: 19% slower.S5
There is no single multiplier to apply. The effect depends on how well you and the codebase already know each other.
Two numbers pull in opposite directions: you under-estimate your own work, and the model over-estimates it. Averaging them is not a method. The method is to use your own recorded history as the correction — the outside view, built from your outcomes rather than anyone's feelings.S6
Division of labour

Claude lists the work. You price it.

The model is genuinely good at the part humans are worst at — remembering that a feature has fourteen pieces, not four. It is unreliable at the part you can measure — how long you take. Split the job along that line.

When to make it plan first, and when not to bother

Anthropic's own guidance is specific about this, and it cuts both ways. Planning is most useful "when you're uncertain about the approach, when the change modifies multiple files, or when you're unfamiliar with the code being modified." And plainly: "If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan."S7 Planning has real overhead; spending it on a one-line change is how estimation ceremony gets a bad name at a client.

No runnable check, no estimable end

The same guidance: "Give Claude a check it can run: tests, a build, a screenshot to compare." Without one, "looks done" is the only signal available and you become the verification loop yourself.S7 That is an estimating problem before it is an engineering one — a task whose finish line only exists in your head cannot be given a duration, because "done" keeps moving.

Keep the estimating session small

Context "fills up fast, and performance degrades as it fills," and a single exploration "might generate and consume tens of thousands of tokens."S7 So do the enumeration in a focused session, hand it your own history deliberately, and take the list out rather than letting it drift into implementation.

✗ Asking the model to price it

"How long will the reply feature take?" → "Roughly two to three weeks."

A stranger's estimate of a generic team, with review cycles you may not have and knowledge you do have. Passing it to a client makes it your commitment.

✓ Asking the model to enumerate it

"List every piece of work this needs, including merge, review and deploy. For each, name what would make it take longer. Do not give me durations."

Now you have a checklist you can price against your own record — and the risks are named, which is what the client actually needs to hear.

The commonest omission

Count all the time, not just the interesting time

Evidence-based scheduling rests on two ideas, and the first one is the one people skip: include all time spent, regardless of relevance. The second is to run a Monte Carlo simulation over your history to produce a probability distribution of completion dates rather than a single date.S8

You do not need the simulation to get most of the benefit. You need the honesty. The hours that vanish from estimates are always the same ones:

  • Waiting on a review, and the round trip after it.
  • The release window — if deploys go out Thursdays, work that finishes Friday ships next week.
  • The client meeting the work exists to be shown in, and preparing for it.
  • Environment and access problems that are nobody's feature.
  • The second attempt, after the first approach turned out to be wrong.

An agent makes the writing faster. It does not make any of the five lines above faster, which is exactly why AI-era estimates miss: the part that got quicker was never the part that dominated.

The core idea

Aggregate the buffer. Never pad the tasks.

Most people's instinct for "be realistic with a client" is to add a bit to every estimate. That instinct produces a schedule that is both later and still late. Here is the documented alternative.

Critical chain project management exists to solve exactly this. It "aggregates the large amounts of safety time added to tasks within a project into the buffers" — a project buffer at the end, feeding buffers where side paths join the main one, and resource buffers — and then monitors progress by "the consumption rate of the buffers rather than individual task performance to schedule."S9

The reason it does that is named outright in the same source: safety left inside individual tasks gets wasted through bad multitasking, student syndrome, Parkinson's Law, and poorly synchronized integration.S9 Two of those are worth knowing by name, because you will watch them happen to you:

Parkinson's Law

"Work expands to fill its available time."S10

Give a two-day task four days and it takes four. The padding is not saved for later; it is spent quietly on polish nobody asked for.

Student syndrome

Planned procrastination — people apply themselves "at the last moment before the deadline," making real progress only once urgency appears.S11

The padded task starts late, so the padding is gone before the work begins — and any real problem now lands with no slack left.

Tasks carry honest middle estimates. The safety lives in one visible block at the end, and only the tasks that actually go wrong consume it. Same total, far better odds — and you can report how much is left.
The buffer belongs to the date, not to the task. That single move is the difference between "we padded everything and still slipped" and "two things went wrong, we spent half the buffer, and the date held."
How big

Size the buffer from your own record, not from a feeling

You already have the raw material if you have been keeping the calibration log on the Delivery Track: what you estimated, what it actually took, and why it missed.

Estimate each part honestly — the middle number, no padding. Aim for something you would beat about half the time.
Add the parts up. That total is the work, and it is not the date.
Multiply your overrun ratio. If your last three items ran at 2.5×, 2.0× and 1.3× your estimate, your history says roughly 2×. The buffer is the difference between the raw total and the corrected one. With no history yet, the research default is about 30% overrun as a floorS1 — and it is a floor, not a ceiling.
Hold it at the end, as one block. Never distribute it back into the tasks. Distributing it is the mistake this whole section exists to prevent.S9
Report consumption, not percentage. "Two of five buffer days used, three left" is a status a client can act on. "About 60% done" is not.S9

The client conversation

How to say it without sounding like you're hedging

The fear is that a buffered date sounds slow, or worse, sounds padded. It reads as the opposite when the buffer is named, sized and reported — because hiding it is what actually looks evasive.

"The work itself is about six days. I'm committing to the 19th, which carries six days of buffer, and I'll tell you every Monday how much of it is left. The two things most likely to eat it are the access approval and the weekly release window. If neither bites, you get this early."

Four things that sentence does. It gives the work estimate and the committed date as different numbers, so nobody confuses effort with delivery. It names the buffer instead of burying it. It names the specific risks, which turns the client into someone who can go unblock one. And it promises a weekly signal, which is the thing that stops a date slipping silently until the week it is due.

✗ Hidden buffer

"Should be about two weeks."

Padded internally, unexplained externally. If it lands early you look like you sandbagged; if it lands late you have no story. Either way the client learns nothing about the risks.

✓ Declared buffer

"Six days of work, committed for the 19th, six days of buffer against the access approval and the release window."

Early delivery is a win you predicted. A late one has a named cause you flagged on day one. The buffer is protection for their date, and that is how you should say it.

If a client pushes back on the buffer, do not delete it — trade it. "I can commit to the 15th if the access approval is pre-cleared, because that's the risk the buffer is mostly covering." Now the date and the risk move together, which is the honest relationship between them.
The alternative, and why it fails

"We'll just work late to make the date"

This is the plan that replaces a buffer, and it is worth knowing the evidence because you will be in a room where somebody proposes it — possibly you.

Output does not scale with hours. Pencavel's analysis of munition workers found the relationship is nonlinear: below an hours threshold, output is proportional to hours; above it, output rises at a decreasing rate as hours increase — and the findings connect to research on long working hours and accidents and injuries.S12 Past the threshold you are buying progressively less work per hour, and buying error alongside it.

The product suffers, not only the person. Sustained crunch "leads to negative health impacts for game developers and a decrease in the quality of their work, which drives developers out of the industry temporarily or permanently."S13 That is three costs — health, quality, and the person leaving — against one benefit that the previous paragraph says you do not fully receive.

And it is usually nobody's decision. One studio head has argued the drive to crunch often comes from developers themselves, wanting to hit a milestone, which then pressures peers into matching hours or looking like they are slacking — the reason the term "crunch culture" exists.S13 Nobody has to mandate it. A schedule with no declared buffer produces it on its own.

Which is the real argument for the buffer, and it is not a comfort argument. A declared buffer is the mechanism that keeps a date honest without spending the one resource that gets worse the more of it you spend.

Making it a habit

The weekly loop that keeps all of this true

None of the above works once. It works because the numbers feed back. This is the same Monday rhythm as the calibration log, seen from the delivery side.

When
What you do
Monday
Report buffer consumed and buffer left, not percent complete.S9 Report last week's estimate against what it actually took, and why it missed.
When new work lands
Have Claude enumerate the parts, including merge, review and deploy. Price them yourself.S7
Before you commit a date
Apply your own overrun ratio, hold the difference as one buffer, and name the two risks it covers.S9
Every task you finish
Log all the time it took — including the waiting.S8 That log is next month's estimate.
Four weeks of this and you have something rare and quietly valuable: a personal overrun ratio, and a date nobody has to discount. Being believable about time is worth more to a client than being fast.
