How an LLM Actually Spends Its Attention

Week 1 · Day 1 Foundation AI Engineering ~18 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

Every superpower and every failure of an LLM traces back to one finite resource: the context window. Today you learn to think like the model's accountant — to work out what the system can actually see when it answers, and what it's quietly missing. That diagnosis is the first thing a Forward Deployed Engineer does in week one at a client. Scroll the reading; the panel on the right assembles one real request into the window slice by slice, then shows what happens when it overflows.

The whole game

One window holds everything

Start with the fact that reframes everything else: an LLM has no memory between calls and no live access to your databases, your filesystem, or the internet. Each request is a blank slate. Whatever you want the model to reason about has to be placed, as text, into a single bounded space called the context window — and then the model reads that space and produces a continuation.

That window is measured in tokens. A token is a chunk of text — roughly ¾ of an English word, or about four characters. "Tokenization" is one token; a long or rare word might split into three or four. The window has a hard ceiling (say 8K, 128K, or 200K tokens depending on the model), and nothing outside it exists from the model's point of view. There is no "it sort of remembers" — either a fact is in the window for this call, or it is gone. Get a feel for the conversion before we keep going:

Here's the sentence to tattoo on your brain for the program: capabilities and limits both come from the window. "Can it summarize this contract?" "Why did it forget what I told it?" "Why did it confidently make something up?" — those feel like different problems, but they are the same question wearing different clothes: how is the finite window being spent, and what didn't make it in?

That single reframe is most of what separates an engineer from someone who pokes at a chatbox until it behaves. The window is not a vague "amount of memory" the model has; it is a literal, countable, billable buffer you fill and the model reads, once, per call. Master that and the rest of the day — system prompts, retrieval, history, sampling, hallucination — stops being a list of disconnected facts and becomes one accounting problem you can reason about on a whiteboard in front of a client.

Slice 1

The system prompt always goes first

The system prompt is the standing instruction set: the model's role, its rules, the output format you require, the tone, the guardrails ("never give medical advice", "always return JSON"). It is prepended to every request, which has a consequence people miss — you pay its token cost on every single call.

A 2,000-token system prompt feels free when you're testing in a playground. Ship it on a feature handling a million requests a day and it's two billion tokens of pure overhead — real latency on every response and a real line on the invoice. This is your first FDE instinct: a system prompt is not a place to dump everything you can think of. It's prime real estate, and every line competes with the user's actual need for space. (Later this week you'll meet prompt caching, which lets providers discount that repeated prefix — but the discipline of keeping it tight comes first.)

There's a second, quieter cost. Every token of standing instruction is a token that isn't available for the user's documents or the model's answer — the system prompt and the payload draw from the same fixed pool. A bloated prompt doesn't just cost money; it shrinks the room left for the work. So the first thing you'll do on many engagements is read the client's existing system prompt and ask, line by line, "does this earn its tokens?" Half of it is usually wishful repetition the model already follows.

Slice 2

Few-shot examples buy quality with tokens

One of the most reliable ways to improve output is to show, don't tell: include a few worked examples of input → desired output right in the prompt. This is "few-shot" prompting (versus "zero-shot", where you only describe the task). Two or three good examples will often lock in a format or tone far more reliably than a paragraph of instructions trying to describe that same format in words.

The catch is that every example is more tokens in the window, on every call, forever. And the benefit has diminishing returns — going from zero to two examples is transformative; going from eight to twelve usually isn't. So the FDE question is never "do examples help?" (they do) but "how few examples can I get away with and still hit the quality bar?" Buying quality deliberately against a budget, rather than spending freely, is the through-line of the whole day.

Slice 3

Retrieved context can dominate

Now the big consumer. When you paste in documents, source files, database rows, or the results of a search, that retrieved context is usually the largest single thing in the window — often by an order of magnitude. This is the heart of RAG (retrieval-augmented generation), which you'll spend all of Week 2 on. For today, one principle matters.

More retrieved text is not more intelligence. It is more budget spent — and worse, it can actively hurt. Stuff twenty documents into the window when only one is relevant and you've done three bad things at once: paid for nineteen useless documents, pushed the answer-room smaller, and buried the signal in noise so the model is likelier to latch onto the wrong passage. The skill you're building toward is precision of retrieval — getting the right small slice in, not the most slices in. Volume is the amateur move.

There's also a subtler trap called "lost in the middle": models attend most reliably to the start and end of a long context and can quietly skim what's buried in the center. So a fact that's technically in the window isn't guaranteed to be used — another reason a tight, well-ordered context beats a giant one. Keep this in your pocket: when a client's answer quality drops the moment they "give the AI more documents," you're almost certainly watching precision collapse under volume, and the fix is better retrieval, not a bigger window.

Slice 4 · the silent leak

Conversation history grows every turn — until it overflows

Chat interfaces hide a sharp edge. The model has no memory, remember — so to make a conversation feel continuous, the application re-sends the entire prior conversation on every new turn. Turn one sends a little. Turn twenty sends everything from turns one through nineteen, plus the new message. Token cost per turn grows as the conversation grows, so a long session quietly gets slower and more expensive the longer it runs.

And there's a failure mode hiding in it. When the accumulated history would overflow the window, something has to give — usually the oldest tokens get dropped off the front. Those oldest tokens are often your system prompt and the user's original instructions.

That is why "the assistant was great at first but forgot the rules halfway through the long chat" is one of the most common complaints you'll hear — and why it's almost never a model-quality problem. It's history eviction: the rules fell out of the window. You'll fix it by managing the history, not by reaching for a bigger model.

Slice 5

The user's question is just one more slice

Notice how late in the stack the user's actual question finally arrives. By the time real intent enters the window, the system prompt, the examples, the retrieved context, and the history may already have eaten most of the available space. The thing the user cares about most is, in token terms, often one of the smallest pieces — and it's competing for room with everything that came before it.

This is worth sitting with, because it inverts the beginner's instinct. Beginners obsess over phrasing the question perfectly. Practitioners obsess over what else is in the room when that question gets asked. A merely-okay question with the right context beats a beautifully-worded question with the wrong context, every time.

This is the lever behind a phrase you'll hear all program — context engineering. Prompt wording is a thin slice of the job; assembling the right system prompt, the right examples, and above all the right retrieved context around the question is the thick part. When two teams get wildly different results from "the same model," the difference is almost always here, in what each team chose to put in the window beside the question.

Token budget

What's left is room to answer

Here's the part that ties it together. The window is shared between input and output — they draw from the same pool. Everything we've stacked up (system prompt + examples + context + history + question) is input. Whatever tokens you didn't spend on input are the room left for the answer. Overstuff the input and the model is forced to truncate, rush, or stop mid-sentence, because there's simply no room left to think.

A token budget is the discipline of deciding, in advance, how many tokens each slice is allowed — so the answer always has the space it needs. "System prompt ≤ 800, retrieved context ≤ 4,000, reserve 1,500 for the answer," and so on. And because every token is metered — cost ≈ (input tokens + output tokens) × price-per-token — that same budget is also your cost model. When a client asks "why is this AI feature so expensive to run?", the honest answer lives somewhere in this picture. Token budgeting is cost engineering wearing a different hat.

Set the reserve first, then fit the inputs around it — the reverse of the beginner's instinct, which is to cram in context until something breaks and let the answer take whatever's left. Decide the answer needs 1,500 tokens, subtract that from the ceiling, and now you have a hard budget to spend on system prompt, examples, and retrieval. That one habit prevents the most common production failure of all: an answer that stops mid-sentence because the input ate the room it needed to finish. The same arithmetic, done out loud, is also how you give a client a credible monthly cost estimate before you've written a line of code.

Generation

Sampling decides how it answers

So far we've talked about what goes in. Now the output. The model doesn't write a whole answer at once — it generates one token at a time, and at each step it produces a probability distribution over all possible next tokens, then picks one. How it picks is controlled by a couple of knobs you'll touch constantly. Drag the slider and watch the distribution change:

Temperature scales how much randomness is allowed. Low temperature (near 0) makes the model almost always take the single most likely token — near-deterministic, repeatable, ideal for extraction, classification, and code. High temperature flattens the distribution so less-likely tokens get picked more often — more varied, more "creative", and less repeatable. Top-p (nucleus sampling) is a related knob that limits the choice to the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p.

The FDE reflex: if the same input must produce the same output, turn temperature down. A huge share of "flaky", "inconsistent", "sometimes it works" AI bugs in production are nothing more than sampling left too hot for a task that needed determinism. (Caveat for later: even at temperature 0 you're not guaranteed bit-identical output across hardware and batching — "more deterministic", not "perfectly deterministic". Design your tests for that.)

Failure mode

Hallucination is a window problem first

A hallucination is a confident, fluent, completely wrong answer. They unsettle people because the model sounds exactly as sure when it's wrong as when it's right. But once you have the window model in your head, the single most common cause is obvious: the fact the model needed was never in the window, so it did the only thing it can do — generated the most plausible-sounding text to fill the gap. The model has no built-in way to say "I wasn't given that."

This reframes an entire category of client complaints. "Your AI lies to our customers" is, nine times out of ten, really "your retrieval missed the relevant document" or "the answer got truncated" or "two retrieved passages contradicted each other." Which means you will fix far more hallucinations by managing the window — better retrieval, grounding answers in cited sources, leaving room for the response — than by swapping in a bigger model. Diagnose the window before you touch the model. That habit is the whole point of today.

It also gives you a concrete playbook instead of a shrug. Faced with "the AI made something up," you walk the window: was the needed fact retrieved at all? If it was retrieved, did it survive — or did history eviction or a too-small answer budget truncate it? If it survived, was it buried in the middle of a noisy context? Each question points at a fix you can ship — tighten retrieval, pin the system prompt, raise the answer reserve, re-rank the context — none of which is "swap the model." That diagnostic ladder is exactly what a client is paying an FDE for.

Your tools · AI coding

Claude Code is a window manager

You'll work all program inside Claude Code, and here's the mental model that makes you good at it fast: it is a context-window manager wearing a terminal. Every time it indexes your repo, opens a file, reads an error, or runs a command and feeds the output back, it is making a decision about what goes in the window for the model behind it. Strong results come from curating that context deliberately — pointing it at the three files that matter — not from writing longer and longer prompts. FDE mindset

When an FDE lands at a client, the first real question is never "what model should we use?" It's "what does the system actually have in front of it when it answers — and what is it missing?" You diagnose the window before you reach for the model. Hold onto that; it's the spine of the whole program, and tomorrow — once the window is second nature — we turn the model choice itself into a framework.

Deep dive: tokens, pricing & a worked budget

Optional. ~10 minutes. The numbers behind the picture.

Tokens aren't words. Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ 0.75 English words. Common words are one token; rare or long words ("antidisestablishmentarianism") split into several; code and punctuation tokenize heavier than prose. When you size a budget, count tokens, not words.

The cost formula. Providers bill input and output separately, per million tokens. Say a model charges $3 / M input and $15 / M output (output is usually pricier). Cost of one call = (input_tokens / 1e6 × $3) + (output_tokens / 1e6 × $15).

Worked example — one support-bot turn
  • System prompt: 1,500 tokens
  • Retrieved help-center docs: 2,000 tokens
  • Conversation history: 1,200 tokens
  • User question: 100 tokens
  • Answer (output): 400 tokens

Input = 4,800 tokens → $0.0144. Output = 400 → $0.0060. ≈ $0.02 per turn. Trivial once — until you multiply by 50,000 turns/day = ~$1,000/day, and suddenly that 1,200-token history you never trimmed is a $250/day line item. Thursday's "context compression" topic is, quite literally, a cost-savings project.

Determinism caveat. Even at temperature 0, outputs are not guaranteed bit-identical across hardware, model versions, or batching. Treat "deterministic" as "highly repeatable", and build evals that tolerate tiny variation.
