Retrieval Strategies: Dense, Sparse, Hybrid & Re-ranking

Week 2 · Day 3 Foundation AI Engineering ~18 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

A RAG system is only as good as the slice of text it puts in the window — and the job of choosing that slice is retrieval. Today you learn the two ways a system can find relevant text (dense semantic search and sparse keyword search), why each one fails on the queries the other handles, how to fuse them into one ranked list, and how a second-stage re-ranker turns a wide, recall-heavy candidate set into a small, precise one. This is the machinery behind "why didn't it find the right document?" — the single most common RAG complaint you'll hear at a client. The panel on the right builds the production pipeline one stage at a time.

The whole game

Retrieval is where RAG lives or dies

Yesterday's window lesson left you with one rule: more retrieved text is not more intelligence — precision of retrieval is. Today we open that black box. When a user asks a question, the system has to reach into a corpus of thousands or millions of chunks and pull back the handful that actually answer it. How it decides which chunks are relevant is the whole ballgame. Get retrieval right and a small, cheap model looks brilliant; get it wrong and the best model on earth confidently answers from the wrong passage.

There are two fundamentally different ways to score "is this chunk relevant to this query," and they come from two different views of what text means. Dense (semantic) search compares the meaning of the query and the chunk. Sparse (lexical / keyword) search compares the literal words they share. Here is the fact that organizes the entire day: these two approaches fail on opposite kinds of queries, which is precisely why mature systems run both. That complementarity is the entire motivation for hybrid search.

Keep a running example in your head, the one Weaviate uses: a user asks "How to catch an Alaskan Pollock." A dense system reads "catch" as a concept and may surface documents about fishing technique in general. A sparse system locks onto the literal phrase "Alaskan Pollock" and finds the exact species page. Each one is right about part of the query and blind to the other part. Hold that example — we'll walk it through every stage of the pipeline.

Approach 1

Dense search understands meaning — and only meaning

Dense retrieval turns both the query and every chunk into an embedding — a vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers that positions the text in a "meaning space." Texts that mean similar things land near each other, even when they share no words. So a query about "automobile maintenance" can match a chunk about "car repair," and a question phrased one way can find a document phrased another way. This is the superpower: dense search captures meaning and matches synonyms and paraphrases. It is what makes RAG feel like the system "understands" the question rather than grepping for strings.

But that strength is also the failure mode. Because dense search reasons in meaning-space, it is weak exactly where the literal token matters more than the concept: exact-keyword hits and domain-specific terminology. Product SKUs, error codes, part numbers, drug names, legal citations, a rare proper noun — these are tokens whose identity is the point, and an embedding tends to smear them toward "things that look semantically similar" instead of demanding the exact match. Ask a dense system for "error TS-4471" and it may cheerfully return passages about errors in general, because in meaning-space "TS-4471" isn't very far from "TS-4470."

That is the trade you are buying: dense search reads "catch" in our Pollock query as the fishing concept and pulls general fishing content, while potentially under-weighting the specific phrase the user actually cares about. Brilliant on intent, shaky on exactness.

Approach 2

Sparse search nails exact terms — and only exact terms

Sparse retrieval is the classical information-retrieval approach, the family that includes BM25. It represents text as a sparse vector over the vocabulary — mostly zeros, with weights on the specific terms a document contains — and scores a chunk by how well its terms match the query's terms, adjusted for how rare and how frequent each term is. It is, in spirit, a very smart keyword search. Its strength is the mirror image of dense's weakness: sparse retrieval excels at exact term matching, so the literal phrase "Alaskan Pollock," the SKU, the error code, the rare proper noun all light up reliably.

And its weakness is the mirror image of dense's strength. Sparse search has no notion of meaning. If the user writes "car repair" and the document says "automobile maintenance," sparse search sees zero shared terms and scores it near nothing — it cannot disambiguate or bridge synonyms and paraphrases. In our example, sparse retrieval matches the literal "Alaskan Pollock" perfectly but is oblivious to whether the surrounding query is about fishing, cooking, or conservation; it matched the string, not the intent.

So now you have the full picture and the reason hybrid exists. Dense vectors disambiguate meaning; sparse keyword scoring catches the exact tokens — and each is blind precisely where the other sees. They are not competitors to choose between. They are complementary signals, and the engineer's move is to run both and combine them. That combination is hybrid search, and the rest of the pipeline is about doing the combining well.

The merge problem

You can't just add the two scores together

Running dense and sparse in parallel is easy. Combining their outputs correctly is where naïve implementations quietly break. Here's the trap: BM25 scores and sparse-weight outputs are not normalized to the dense vector's range. A cosine similarity from a dense index typically lives between 0 and 1; a BM25 score can be any positive number — 8, 23, 40 — depending on term rarity and document length. If you simply add the two scores, the sparse component, living on a much larger numeric scale, dominates the combined score and your "hybrid" system is really just sparse search wearing a costume.

So normalization and explicit weighting are mandatory before production, not a nice-to-have. The most common control is an alpha convex combination: you scale both signals into a comparable range and compute alpha · dense + (1 − alpha) · sparse, where alpha is a dial between 0 (pure sparse) and 1 (pure dense). Tuning that single number is one of the highest-leverage things you'll do on a RAG engagement, and the defaults are worth memorizing.

The rules of thumb: alpha ≈ 0.75 is a good default for natural-language queries (lean on meaning), alpha ≈ 0.5 balances both signals evenly, and alpha ≈ 0.25 favors high-keyword-specificity queries — SKUs, technical IDs, named entities — where the literal token matters most. A single product can even pick alpha per query type: conversational questions ride high alpha, while a query that's obviously a part number drops alpha toward sparse. That's not a parlor trick; it's reading the query and spending your retrieval budget where the signal is.

Fusion done right

RRF: fuse by rank, not by score

Weighting works, but it still asks you to make two incompatible score scales commensurable. There's a more robust trick that sidesteps the whole normalization headache: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). Instead of combining raw scores, RRF combines the two ranked lists. Each document gets a fused score from the sum of its reciprocal ranks across the lists — roughly Σ 1 / (k + rank) over each retriever that returned it. A document ranked #1 by dense and #3 by sparse accumulates a strong combined score; a document only one retriever liked, far down its list, contributes little.

The beauty is that rank is unitless. RRF never looks at the magnitude of a BM25 score or a cosine similarity — only at the order each retriever produced. That makes it inherently robust to incompatible score scales between BM25 and dense retrieval, which is exactly the problem that sinks naïve score-summing. It's why RRF is the default fusion method in production stacks: Weaviate ships it as the default rankedFusion, with a score-based relativeScoreFusion available as the alternative when you specifically want magnitudes to matter.

Practically, you'll choose between the two families a lot. Reach for RRF / rank fusion when your two retrievers have wildly different score distributions and you want a safe, low-tuning default. Reach for a weighted score combination (alpha) when you've done the normalization work and want fine, per-query-type control over how much meaning vs keyword matters. Either way the goal is identical: one merged, sensibly-ordered candidate list out of two very different retrievers.

Stage 1 of 2

Hybrid retrieval buys recall — on purpose

Now zoom out to the shape of a real production system, because this is the pattern clients actually run. It's two-stage. Stage one is hybrid retrieval: dense and sparse each pull a wide candidate set — say each returns the top ~100 — and you fuse them. The explicit goal of stage one is recall: cast a wide net so that the truly relevant chunk is somewhere in the candidate pool, even if it's not yet at the top. You are deliberately accepting some junk in exchange for near-certainty that the right answer is in the bucket.

This is a mindset shift worth naming. In stage one you are not trying to be precise. A hundred-plus candidates is far too many to hand a language model — yesterday's lesson tells you stuffing all of them into the window is the amateur move that buries the signal and shrinks the answer room. So why pull so many? Because you can't re-rank a document you never retrieved. Stage one's only job is to make sure nothing important got missed. Precision is somebody else's problem — specifically, stage two's.

The phrase to carry to a client whiteboard: "hybrid boosts recall; reranking restores precision." That single sentence explains the whole architecture and tells a team why both stages exist. Skip stage two and you drown the model in candidates; skip stage one's width and stage two has nothing good to promote. They're a matched pair.

Stage 2 of 2

Re-ranking restores precision

Stage two takes that wide candidate set and collapses it to the small final set the model will actually see — the top 3, 5, or 10. The workhorse here is a cross-encoder re-ranker, and the architectural difference from stage one is the whole point. Dense retrieval embeds the query and the document separately and compares the two vectors — fast, because you precompute every document's embedding once, but coarse, because the model never sees the query and document together. A cross-encoder feeds the query and each candidate document jointly through a transformer and outputs a single, precise relevance score. It can reason about how the query and that specific document interact — which is far more accurate and far too expensive to run across the whole corpus, but perfectly affordable across ~100 candidates.

The production-standard example is Cohere Rerank 3.5: a cross-encoder that takes the query plus candidate documents and returns a relevance-scored ordering, with a 4096-token context window and state-of-the-art results on the BEIR and multilingual retrieval benchmarks. You hand it your fused 100, it hands you back the same documents re-ordered by true relevance, and you keep the top few. That reordering is where the precision the user feels actually comes from.

And the payoff is measurable, not hand-wavy. Pinecone's cascading retrieval runs dense and sparse in parallel, then re-ranks the merged candidates into one unified score; adding the reranker improved performance by up to 48%, averaging 24%, over dense-only vector search. That's the single most important number to take to a skeptical client: the re-ranking stage is not polish, it's often a double-digit accuracy jump for a bounded, cheap-per-query cost. When someone asks "is re-ranking worth the extra hop?", that range is your answer.

Variations & where it runs

Late interaction, and hybrid right in Postgres

Cross-encoders aren't the only re-ranking tool, and you should know the one clients increasingly ask about: late-interaction models, the ColBERT family. Instead of crushing a whole document into one vector, ColBERT stores a per-token embedding for the document and scores a query against it with MaxSim — for each query token, take its best-matching document token, then sum those maxima. This recovers fine-grained linguistic nuance that a single document vector flattens away. Because storing per-token embeddings for an entire corpus is heavy, the practical pattern is the same two-stage shape: retrieve cheaply with single-vector search, then re-rank the candidates with the late-interaction model — getting ColBERT's nuance without scanning the whole corpus token-by-token. Qdrant supports exactly this as a late-interaction rerank stage.

Finally, a grounding fact for the many clients who'd rather not add another database: you can do hybrid natively in Postgres. The pgvector extension ships a sparsevec type for keyword/lexical sparse vectors alongside the halfvec half-precision type — both introduced in pgvector 0.7.0 — which means you can keep a dense vector column and a sparse vector column in the same table and run both halves of hybrid retrieval inside one database. pgvector 0.8.0 also added iterative index scans, which prevent over-filtering by continuing to scan an HNSW or IVFFlat index until enough rows pass your WHERE-clause filters — a real fix for the classic "my metadata filter starved the vector search" bug. (Version hygiene matters: the current stable release as of mid-2026 is pgvector 0.8.3, so any "we're on a current version" claim should point past 0.8.0.)

Step back and you have a portable mental model that survives whichever vendor a client has chosen. Whether it's Pinecone's cascading retrieval, Weaviate's rankedFusion, Qdrant's server-side hybrid with ColBERT rerank, or pgvector columns in plain Postgres, the shape is identical: run dense and sparse, fuse them into a wide high-recall candidate set, then re-rank down to a small high-precision final set. Learn the shape, and every product is just a different set of knobs on the same machine.

FDE mindset

When a client says "our RAG can't find the right document," resist the urge to swap the embedding model. Walk the retrieval pipeline instead: Is sparse even in the mix, or is it dense-only missing the exact terms? Are the two scores normalized, or is BM25 silently dominating? Is the candidate set wide enough to contain the answer? Is there a re-ranking stage at all? Most "bad retrieval" is one of those four knobs set wrong — and each has a fix you can ship this week, none of which is "buy a bigger model."

Deep dive: RRF math & tuning alpha

Optional. ~10 minutes. The arithmetic behind fusion and the dial behind hybrid.

Why raw scores don't add up. A dense cosine similarity sits in a tidy 0–1 range. A BM25 score has no fixed ceiling — it grows with how rare a query term is and how concentrated it is in the document, so it routinely lands at 8, 20, 40. Add them directly and the sparse number simply swamps the dense one. That's the trap: an un-normalized sum is sparse search pretending to be hybrid.

Worked example — Reciprocal Rank Fusion

RRF ignores magnitudes entirely and fuses by rank. With the common constant k = 60, a document's fused score is the sum over each list of 1 / (k + rank):

  • Doc A — dense rank 1, sparse rank 30 → 1/61 + 1/90 ≈ 0.0164 + 0.0111 = 0.0275
  • Doc B — dense rank 4, sparse rank 2 → 1/64 + 1/62 ≈ 0.0156 + 0.0161 = 0.0317
  • Doc C — dense rank 2, not in sparse list → 1/62 + 0 ≈ 0.0161

Doc B wins — not because either retriever ranked it #1, but because both ranked it high. Notice no BM25 magnitude ever entered the math; that's why RRF shrugs off incompatible score scales and is the sane default fusion method.

Tuning alpha by query type. If you use weighted fusion instead, treat alpha as a per-query-type dial: ≈0.75 for conversational natural-language questions (lean on meaning), ≈0.5 to balance, and ≈0.25 when the query is dominated by exact tokens — SKUs, error codes, part numbers, named entities. A lightweight classifier (or even a regex that detects ID-shaped queries) can pick alpha per request, so "reset my password" rides high alpha while "TS-4471" drops toward sparse.

Production code: hybrid retrieval + re-ranking

Query time: fuse a dense Titan vector search with a sparse (BM25) lexical search, then Bedrock's Rerank API re-ranks the shortlist to restore precision.

# Query time: hybrid (dense Titan + BM25) fusion, then Bedrock re-rank.
import boto3

agent = boto3.client("bedrock-agent-runtime", region_name="us-east-1")
RERANK_ARN = "arn:aws:bedrock:us-east-1::foundation-model/amazon.rerank-v1:0"

def hybrid_search(q: str, k: int = 20, alpha: float = 0.5) -> list[dict]:
    qv = embed(q)                    # Titan V2, same model as ingest
    dense = dense_search(qv, k)      # pgvector: ORDER BY embedding <=> qv
    sparse = bm25_search(q, k)       # lexical: exact terms, part numbers
    fused = fuse(dense, sparse, alpha)[:k]   # alpha dials dense-vs-sparse

    out = agent.rerank(
        queries=[{"type": "TEXT", "textQuery": {"text": q}}],
        sources=[{"type": "INLINE", "inlineDocumentSource":
                  {"type": "TEXT", "textDocument": {"text": d["body"]}}} for d in fused],
        rerankingConfiguration={"type": "BEDROCK_RERANKING_MODEL",
            "bedrockRerankingConfiguration": {"numberOfResults": 5,
                "modelConfiguration": {"modelArn": RERANK_ARN}}},
    )
    return [fused[r["index"]] | {"score": r["relevanceScore"]} for r in out["results"]]

The reranker reads (query, doc) together, so it's precise but costly -- that's why it only runs on the fused top-k shortlist, never the whole corpus. amazon.rerank-v1:0 is called through the bedrock-agent-runtime Rerank API.
