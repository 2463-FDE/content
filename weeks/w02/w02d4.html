RAG Evaluation: Proving the Pipeline Actually Works

Week 2 · Day 4 Foundation AI Engineering ~18 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

You've built a RAG system. It returns answers. They look right. That is exactly the moment a Forward Deployed Engineer should get nervous — because "looks right" is not "is right," and the client is about to bet a business process on it. Today you learn to measure a RAG pipeline the way the field actually does it: splitting it into a retrieval half and a generation half, and scoring each with metrics that turn vibes into numbers. By the end you can tell a client not just that the system is wrong, but where it broke and which knob to turn. Scroll the reading; the panel on the right assembles the evaluation pipeline one metric at a time.

The whole game

A RAG system is two systems, so it needs two scorecards

Every RAG pipeline does two distinct jobs in sequence. First it retrieves: given a question, it pulls some set of chunks out of a knowledge base. Then it generates: it hands those chunks to an LLM and asks it to write an answer. These are different machines with different failure modes, and the single biggest mistake in RAG evaluation is scoring them as one blob. A great answer from bad retrieval is luck; a bad answer from perfect retrieval is a generation bug. You cannot fix either one until you know which one you have.

The field has converged on three core metrics that map cleanly onto the three things a RAG system must get right: rank the useful chunks high, retrieve all the chunks you need, and ground the answer in those chunks. Those three are context precision, context recall, and faithfulness. The first two live on the retrieval side; the last lives on the generation side. Keep that split in your head all day — it is the spine of every diagnosis you will run.

Why does an FDE care so much about measurement specifically? Because on an engagement you do not get to say "the AI is pretty good." You get asked "can we trust it to answer customer billing questions without a human in the loop?" — and the only honest answer is a number with a method behind it. Evaluation is what converts a demo into a system you can sign your name to. It is also what lets you change the pipeline next week and prove you made it better instead of just different.

Retrieval · metric 1

Context recall: did we even fetch the right information?

Start on the retrieval side, with the most fundamental question of all: was the information the answer needs actually in the chunks we retrieved? That is context recall. If the relevant passage never made it into the context, nothing downstream can save you — the generator is being asked to answer from material that doesn't contain the answer, and a confident hallucination is the predictable result.

Ragas computes context recall against a reference answer (the ground truth you supply). It breaks that reference answer into individual claims, then checks each claim for whether it can be attributed to the retrieved context. The score is the fraction of reference claims supported by what was retrieved: (reference claims supported by the retrieved context) / (total claims in the reference). A recall of 1.0 means every fact the gold answer relies on was present somewhere in the chunks you pulled; a recall of 0.4 means more than half the needed information never showed up.

This is the metric that tells you whether your retrieval architecture is fundamentally sound. Low recall is not a tuning problem you can prompt your way out of — it means your chunking, your embeddings, your index, or your top-k is dropping information the system genuinely requires. When recall is low, no amount of clever generation prompting will help, because the raw material isn't there. That is why context recall, along with faithfulness, is one of the two highest-signal metrics for most production systems: it directly answers "is the retrieval layer good enough to build on?"

Retrieval · metric 2

Context precision: are the good chunks ranked at the top?

Recall asks whether the needed chunks were retrieved. Context precision asks a sharper question: are the relevant chunks ranked at the top, ahead of the irrelevant ones? A retriever that returns the right passage as result number nine, buried under eight pieces of noise, technically "found" it — but it has spent your context budget on garbage and pushed the signal to where the generator is least likely to use it well.

Ragas computes context precision as mean precision@k across the retrieved chunks, where precision@k = true positives@k / (true positives@k + false positives@k). The crucial nuance: position matters. An irrelevant chunk ranked first hurts the score far more than the same irrelevant chunk ranked lower down. This is deliberate — it mirrors reality, where junk at the top of the context does the most damage to both cost and answer quality. A high-precision retriever puts the wheat first and the chaff last (or, ideally, leaves the chaff out entirely).

Ragas ships this metric in a few flavours you will choose between on real work. One scores chunks against a ground-truth reference. A second is reference-free and instead compares each chunk to the generated response, which is useful when you have no labelled answers. A third is a non-LLM variant that matches retrieved chunk ids against known-relevant ids — cheap and deterministic when you have that labelling. The reference-free variant is the one you reach for first on a client engagement where nobody has built a labelled test set yet.

Generation · the headline metric

Faithfulness: is the answer actually grounded in the context?

Now cross to the generation side. Suppose retrieval did its job and the right information is sitting in the context. The generator can still betray you by writing things the context does not support — adding a detail it "knows" from pretraining, blending two facts into a false third, or simply making something up. Faithfulness is the metric that catches this, and it is the single most important score in most production RAG systems because it measures the most dangerous failure mode: hallucination.

Faithfulness measures how factually consistent a response is with the retrieved context, on a 0–1 scale. The recipe: extract the individual claims made in the response, then verify each one against the retrieved context. A claim counts as supported if the context backs it up (DeepEval frames the same idea from the other direction — a claim is "truthful" if it does not contradict the retrieval context). The score is (number of supported claims) / (total claims in the response). A faithfulness of 1.0 means every single statement in the answer can be traced back to the source material; 0.7 means roughly a third of the answer's claims are floating free of any grounding.

DeepEval computes faithfulness the same way and makes the operational details concrete: it requires the input, the actual output and the retrieval context, and it applies a default pass threshold of 0.5. A strict mode forces binary scoring at a threshold of 1, meaning anything less than perfectly grounded fails. That threshold is a policy decision you make with the client: a customer-facing medical or financial assistant might demand strict mode, while an internal brainstorming tool can tolerate a softer bar.

The shared engine

How the scoring actually works: LLM-as-judge

You may have noticed that faithfulness, context recall, and context precision all share the same underlying move: break text into claims, then verify each claim. That shared mechanism is LLM-as-judge evaluation — instead of comparing strings exactly (which fails the moment the model phrases a correct answer differently), an LLM extracts the claims and judges whether each is supported. This is what lets these metrics work on free-form natural-language answers at all; exact-match scoring would call a perfectly correct paraphrase "wrong."

The catch is cost and consistency. Running an LLM judge over every claim in every response, across a test set of thousands of examples, is slow and expensive — and the judge is itself a model that can be wrong. The field has responses to both problems. For faithfulness specifically, Ragas offers a FaithfulnesswithHHEM variant that swaps the per-claim LLM calls for Vectara's HHEM-2.1-Open, a T5-based classifier purpose-built to check whether one piece of text is supported by another. It performs the claim-verification step far more cheaply than calling a general-purpose LLM once per claim, which matters enormously when you are scoring a large eval set on every change.

The FDE takeaway: these metrics are not magic oracles, they are LLMs (or small classifiers) grading other LLMs. Treat them as instruments with their own error bars. You validate the judge against a small set of human-labeled examples, you pick the cheaper NLI classifier when you're scoring at volume, and you never present a single eval number to a client without understanding how it was produced.

Robustness

Noise sensitivity: what happens when the context is messy?

The three core metrics assume a fairly clean setup. Real retrieval is messier — it returns a mix of relevant and irrelevant chunks, and a robust system should stay correct anyway. Noise sensitivity measures exactly this: how often the system produces incorrect claims when given relevant or irrelevant retrieved documents. It is computed as (incorrect claims) / (total claims in the response), and unlike the metrics so far, lower is better — it's an error rate.

Why does this deserve its own metric when faithfulness already exists? Because they catch different things. Faithfulness asks whether the answer is grounded in the context that was retrieved. Noise sensitivity probes whether the presence of noise in that context knocks the system off course — whether throwing in irrelevant documents causes the generator to pick up false claims it otherwise wouldn't. A system can be reasonably faithful on clean inputs and still be fragile: add three off-topic chunks and its error rate spikes. That fragility is invisible until you measure it, and it's precisely the kind of thing that surfaces in production when retrieval quality drifts.

For an FDE, noise sensitivity is a stress test. Clients rarely have pristine knowledge bases — they have duplicated docs, outdated pages, and near-miss chunks that look relevant but aren't. Measuring noise sensitivity tells you how much the system's correctness depends on perfect retrieval, which in turn tells you how much engineering effort to spend hardening the retriever versus hardening the generator's instructions to ignore irrelevant context.

The taxonomy

The seven failure points: a shared map of how RAG breaks

Metrics give you numbers; a taxonomy gives you names for what those numbers mean. The canonical one comes from an experience report, "Seven Failure Points When Engineering a RAG System," and it is worth memorizing because it gives you and the client a shared vocabulary for triage. The seven points are: FP1 Missing Content (the answer simply isn't in the knowledge base), FP2 Missed the Top Ranked Documents (it's in the base but didn't get retrieved into the top results), FP3 Not in Context (retrieved, but lost to consolidation or context-window limits before generation), FP4 Not Extracted (present in the context, but the LLM failed to pull it out), FP5 Wrong Format, FP6 Incorrect Specificity (too vague or too detailed for the question), and FP7 Incomplete (partial answer, missing pieces).

Here is the connection that makes the taxonomy operational: it maps onto the retrieval/generation split you've been building all day. FP1 and FP2 are retrieval-side failures — the kind context recall and context precision are designed to detect. FP4, "Not Extracted," is a generation-side failure that is easy to confuse with retrieval failure: the answer is sitting in the context, but the model didn't use it. That distinction is exactly why you must report retrieval and generation metrics separately. If you only looked at end-to-end correctness, an FP4 and an FP1 look identical — both are wrong answers — but the fix could not be more different. FP1 means rebuild retrieval; FP4 means fix the prompt or the generator.

This is the payoff of the whole day. A wrong answer is not a diagnosis, it's a symptom. The seven failure points plus the metric split let you walk a misbehaving RAG system the way you learned to walk the context window in Week 1: was the content in the base at all (FP1)? Did it get retrieved and ranked (FP2, context precision)? Did it survive into the context (FP3)? Did the model extract it (FP4, faithfulness)? Each question points at a different fix, and being able to name the failure in front of a client is a large part of what they are paying an FDE for.

What to actually do

Prioritize: faithfulness and recall first

You now have a toolbox of metrics, which raises a real risk: drowning the client in a dashboard of twelve numbers nobody acts on. So lead with the two that carry the most signal. For most production systems, faithfulness and context recall are the highest-signal metrics: faithfulness catches hallucination — the most dangerous failure mode, the one that erodes trust fastest — and context recall tells you whether the retrieval architecture is fundamentally sound enough to build on at all. If those two are healthy, you have a system worth refining. If either is broken, you have a system worth rebuilding, and refining anything else is rearranging deck chairs.

Around that core, add context precision when cost and latency matter (it tells you how much budget you're wasting on junk chunks) and noise sensitivity when the knowledge base is messy or drifting. Report retrieval-side and generation-side scores in separate columns, always — that's the discipline that keeps an FP4 from being misdiagnosed as an FP1. And remember the tooling reality from earlier: Ragas (PyPI package ragas, with its canonical repo now under the vibrantlabsai GitHub org) and DeepEval both implement these metrics off the shelf, so you are wiring up well-defined instruments, not inventing scoring from scratch.

The mental shift to carry out of today is the same one that separates an engineer from someone poking at a chatbox: you don't ship a RAG system, you ship a RAG system plus the evaluation that proves it works and will keep working. The eval set is what lets you change embeddings, swap a model, or re-chunk the corpus next month and demonstrate — with numbers, split by stage — that you made it better. That artifact, more than any single clever retrieval trick, is what a client is actually buying when they hire someone to make their RAG system trustworthy.

FDE mindset

When a client says "the AI gave a wrong answer," the amateur swaps the model. The FDE asks: was this a retrieval failure or a generation failure? Context recall and precision answer the first; faithfulness answers the second; the seven failure points give the answer a name. Diagnose the stage before you touch the knob — and never present the diagnosis without the eval that backs it.

Deep dive: how claim-based scoring actually runs

Optional. ~10 minutes. The mechanism behind the numbers.

Why not just string-match? Exact-match scoring breaks the instant a model phrases a correct answer differently from your reference — "the policy renews annually" vs "it's a yearly renewal" would score as wrong. So Ragas and DeepEval evaluate claims, not strings: an LLM extracts the discrete factual statements from a piece of text, and then each statement is checked for support. This is the shared engine — LLM-as-judge — under faithfulness, context recall, and context precision alike.

Worked example — scoring one answer

Response: "Refunds take 5 business days and are issued to the original card; store credit is also available." Suppose the retrieved context supports the 5-business-days claim and the original-card claim, but says nothing about store credit.

  • Claim 1 — "refunds take 5 business days" → supported ✓
  • Claim 2 — "issued to the original card" → supported ✓
  • Claim 3 — "store credit is available" → not in context ✗

Faithfulness = 2 / 3 ≈ 0.67. Under DeepEval's default threshold of 0.5 this passes; under strict_mode (threshold 1) it fails, because one claim is ungrounded. The store-credit line is a textbook hallucination — fluent, plausible, and unsupported.

The cost lever. Extracting and verifying every claim with a general LLM, over thousands of test cases, gets expensive fast. Ragas' FaithfulnesswithHHEM swaps the verification step for Vectara's HHEM-2.1-Open, a small T5-based classifier built to judge whether one text is supported by another — much cheaper per claim, which is what makes scoring a large eval set on every code change practical rather than aspirational.

Production code: the Ragas eval harness

Offline evaluation with Ragas, judged by Claude on Bedrock with Titan embeddings -- scoring retrieval and generation separately so the diagnosis is unambiguous.

# Offline eval with Ragas -- judged by Claude on Bedrock + Titan embeddings.
from ragas import evaluate
from ragas.metrics import (
    context_recall, context_precision,   # retrieval quality
    faithfulness, answer_relevancy,       # generation quality
)
from ragas.llms import LangchainLLMWrapper
from ragas.embeddings import LangchainEmbeddingsWrapper
from langchain_aws import ChatBedrockConverse, BedrockEmbeddings
from datasets import Dataset

# bedrock_api_key sets AWS_BEARER_TOKEN_BEDROCK under the hood -- no IAM creds.
# The judge is where we deliberately SIZE UP to Sonnet 4.6: eval quality is worth
# the extra cost. Everywhere else in the pipeline we default to Haiku 4.5.
judge = LangchainLLMWrapper(ChatBedrockConverse(
    model="us.anthropic.claude-sonnet-4-6-v1", region_name="us-east-1"))
emb = LangchainEmbeddingsWrapper(BedrockEmbeddings(
    model_id="amazon.titan-embed-text-v2:0", region_name="us-east-1"))

def run_eval(samples: list[dict]):
    # each sample: {question, answer, contexts, ground_truth}
    ds = Dataset.from_list(samples)
    return evaluate(ds, llm=judge, embeddings=emb, metrics=[
        context_recall, context_precision, faithfulness, answer_relevancy,
    ])

Read it stage-split: low recall means fix the retriever; high recall but low faithfulness means fix generation (re-ranking + grounding), not retrieval.
