RAG: Giving the Model a Library Card

Week 2 · Day 1 Foundation AI Engineering ~17 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

Last week you learned that the model can only reason over what sits inside its context window. This week you learn how to choose what goes in there when the source material is far too big to paste. That is retrieval-augmented generation — RAG — and it is the single most common thing an FDE builds at a client. Today is the foundation: what RAG is, when to reach for it instead of fine-tuning, and the unglamorous-but-decisive craft of chunking — cutting documents into pieces small enough to retrieve and large enough to mean something. Scroll the reading; the panel on the right assembles a real RAG pipeline stage by stage, from raw corpus to a grounded answer.

The whole game

Two kinds of memory, joined

An LLM ships with everything it knows baked into its weights during training. That is parametric memory — frozen at a cutoff date, impossible to update without retraining, and with no idea what's in your company's wiki, ticket history, or contracts. RAG fixes this by bolting on a second kind of memory: a non-parametric store — an external index of your documents that you can search at query time and read fresh into the window.

The original RAG paper put it precisely: combine a pre-trained parametric model with a non-parametric memory (a dense vector index of an external corpus), and the system produces "more specific, diverse and factual language" than the same model working from its weights alone. That phrase is the whole pitch. RAG doesn't make the model smarter; it makes the model better informed for this one answer, by placing the right source text in front of it.

Here's the mental model to carry through the week: RAG is a library card, not a brain transplant. The model is still the same reader it always was. What changes is that, instead of answering from memory, it first walks to the right shelf, pulls the relevant pages, and answers with those pages open on the desk. Every design decision today — how you cut the books into pages, how you index them, how you find the right ones — is in service of that one moment: getting the correct small slice of your corpus into the window beside the question.

And because retrieval feeds the window, everything from last week still applies. Retrieved context is budget spent. Precision beats volume. The fact has to actually make it into the window to be used. RAG is the discipline of winning the context-engineering problem you met on Day 1, at the scale of millions of tokens you could never paste by hand.

The first decision

Retrieval vs fine-tuning — and when to skip both

Before you build anything, you make a choice that clients constantly get wrong: should this knowledge live in retrieval or in fine-tuning? They are not the same tool. Fine-tuning continues training the model on your data, baking new behavior into the weights. Retrieval leaves the weights untouched and supplies knowledge at runtime from an index you control.

The dividing line is mostly about how often the knowledge changes. Retrieval is the right call for dynamic, frequently-changing knowledge and broad knowledge bases, because you can add or correct information in the index without retraining anything — change a document, re-index it, done. Fine-tuning bakes knowledge into weights, which makes it slow and expensive to update but excellent at stylistic and domain nuance: a house voice, a legal register, a specialized jargon the base model fumbles. The mature answer is usually not "either/or" but "both" — fine-tune for how the model should sound, use RAG for the facts it must get right today. When a client says "we fine-tuned a model on our docs and it still gives stale answers," you've found a team that used the wrong tool for changing facts.

There's a third option people forget, and it's often the correct one: do neither. If the entire knowledge base fits in the context window, you can skip retrieval and just put the whole corpus in the prompt. Anthropic's guidance draws the line concretely — for knowledge bases under roughly 200,000 tokens (about 500 pages), paste it all in; RAG and chunking become necessary only when the corpus exceeds what the window can hold. This matters because RAG is real engineering with real failure modes. Don't build a retrieval pipeline for a 40-page employee handbook. The FDE move is to size the corpus first, then reach for the simplest thing that fits.

The pipeline

Ingestion: load, split, embed, index

Once you've decided the corpus is too big to paste, you build an ingestion pipeline — the offline process that turns a pile of documents into a searchable index. It has four stages, and every RAG system you'll ever touch is some version of these four: load the raw documents (PDFs, HTML, wiki pages, database rows) into text; split that text into chunks; embed each chunk into a vector; and index those vectors into a store you can query by similarity.

The reason to see it as a pipeline — rather than a black box — is that quality problems are almost always traceable to one specific stage. A retrieval system returning garbage is rarely "the embeddings are bad." It's far more often that the load step mangled tables into nonsense, or the split step cut a sentence in half, or the index step is doing the wrong distance math. Knowing the four stages gives you a checklist to walk when retrieval misbehaves, exactly like walking the window did for hallucinations last week.

The decisive stage — the one that quietly determines how good the whole system can ever be — is split. Everything downstream inherits its choices. Embeddings can only represent the chunks you give them; retrieval can only return the chunks you stored. If a chunk is a meaningless fragment, no amount of clever indexing rescues it. So we spend the heart of today on splitting, which has a proper name: chunking.

Chunking

Three families of chunking

Chunking is cutting documents into pieces that are small enough to retrieve precisely and large enough to carry self-contained meaning. That tension is the whole art: chunks too big and you retrieve a wall of text where one paragraph was relevant (and you blow the budget, and you bury the signal — last week's lesson, again); chunks too small and you shred the context so badly that no single piece answers anything. There are three core families, and the right one depends on your document structure and query patterns.

Fixed-size is the simplest: cut every N characters or tokens, with an optional overlap. It's fast, predictable, and completely blind to meaning — it will happily slice mid-sentence. It's a fine baseline and sometimes all you need.

Recursive is the recommended default for most prose. Instead of cutting blindly, it tries to split along a hierarchy of natural boundaries — paragraphs first, then lines, then words, then individual characters — only descending to the next level when a chunk is still too big. LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter does exactly this, with the default separator order ["\n\n", "\n", " ", ""]: paragraphs, then lines, then words, then characters. The effect is that it keeps semantically-related text together as long as possible, only breaking finer when it has to. For ordinary documents this is the one you reach for first.

Semantic chunking is the most sophisticated: it embeds individual sentences and groups them by similarity, so a chunk boundary falls where the topic shifts rather than where a character count runs out. It produces the most context-aware chunks, at the cost of more computation up front. Reach for it when document structure is messy and topic coherence matters more than speed.

The seam

Overlap: stitching context across the cut

Whichever family you pick, you've created a new problem the moment you cut: a fact can land across a boundary. The chunk ends "...the maximum retry count is" and the next chunk begins "5, after which the job is marked failed." Retrieve either chunk alone and the answer is broken in half. This is the single most common chunking failure, and it has a standard remedy: chunk overlap.

Overlap means each chunk repeats the last slice of the previous one, so context that straddles a boundary survives in at least one chunk intact. It exists specifically to mitigate information loss when context is split across chunk boundaries. The common guidance is to set overlap to 10–20% of the chunk size — for a 500-token chunk, that's 50–100 tokens of overlap. Too little and you reopen the seam problem; too much and you're storing and paying for the same text many times over, bloating the index for diminishing returns. It's the same buy-quality-against-a-budget reasoning from Day 1, applied to the index this time.

Notice how the parameters compound: chunk size and overlap together decide how many chunks you produce, how much each one means in isolation, and how much redundancy you carry. There is no universal "correct" setting — it depends on your documents and the questions people ask of them. The FDE habit is to treat chunk size and overlap as tunable knobs you evaluate, not constants you copy from a tutorial. Later this week you'll measure retrieval quality directly and tune these against numbers instead of vibes.

Embeddings

Turning chunks into vectors — and why dimensions matter

Each chunk now gets turned into an embedding: a list of numbers — a vector — that captures the chunk's meaning so that semantically similar text lands near it in vector space. This is what makes retrieval "understand" that a query about "resetting my password" should match a chunk about "credential recovery" even with no shared words. Retrieval is, mechanically, finding the stored vectors nearest the query's vector.

The vector's length — its number of dimensions — is a real engineering lever, not a detail. OpenAI's current models illustrate the tradeoff: text-embedding-3-large defaults to 3072 dimensions and text-embedding-3-small to 1536. More dimensions can capture more nuance but cost more to store and compare across millions of chunks. Both models support a dimensions parameter — based on a technique called Matryoshka representation — that lets you shorten the vectors while keeping most of their quality. Strikingly, a 256-dimension text-embedding-3-large vector still beats a full 1536-dimension ada-002 on the MTEB benchmark. The lesson: bigger isn't automatically better, and you can often trade a little accuracy for a large reduction in storage and latency — a classic FDE cost-vs-quality dial.

One rule that trips up every beginner: the same embedding model must be used for both ingestion and queries. Vectors from two different models live in different, incompatible spaces — nearest-neighbour math between them is meaningless. Pick a model, embed your whole corpus with it, and embed every query with it too. Changing embedding models later means re-embedding the entire corpus.

The store

Where vectors live: the vector index

The vectors need a home that can answer "which stored vectors are nearest this query vector?" quickly, across potentially millions of entries. That's a vector store, and you'll meet two common shapes of it in the field: a vector extension on a database you already run, and a purpose-built vector database.

pgvector turns Postgres into a vector store — attractive because the client already operates Postgres and doesn't want a new system to babysit. It offers two approximate-nearest-neighbour index types: HNSW, which gives a better speed/recall tradeoff, needs no training step, and can even be built on an empty table; and IVFFlat. You create one like CREATE INDEX ON items USING hnsw (embedding vector_l2_ops) WITH (m = 16, ef_construction = 64);, and you choose a distance operator to match your embeddings — <-> for L2, <=> for cosine, <#> for negative inner product. Crucially, without an index pgvector falls back to exact search — correct, but slow at scale; the ANN index is what makes it fast. That distinction — exact vs approximate — is one you'll explain to clients often.

Chroma represents the purpose-built option, and it's notable for how much it does for you. You attach an embedding function to a collection, and Chroma applies it transparently on add, update, upsert, and query — you hand it text, it handles the vectors. If you don't specify a function it uses a sensible default: DefaultEmbeddingFunction, which is Sentence Transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 384-dimension model that runs locally with no API key. That makes it excellent for prototyping — but note the dimension gap with the OpenAI models above, and remember the don't-mix-embedders rule when you graduate from prototype to production.

Retrieval quality

Hybrid retrieval: two ways of finding, fused

Now the payoff stage. A query comes in, you embed it, and you find the nearest chunks. But dense vector search has a blind spot: it's brilliant at meaning and weak at exact terms — product codes, error strings, names, rare jargon — where the literal token is what matters. The fix is hybrid retrieval: run dense embedding search alongside classic lexical search (BM25) and fuse the results. Hybrid outperforms either alone because embeddings catch semantic matches while BM25 catches exact term and phrase matches. The two methods cover each other's weaknesses.

You can push retrieval quality further still, and Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval gives concrete numbers worth memorizing. The technique prepends chunk-specific context to each chunk before embedding and BM25 indexing — so a chunk that just says "the limit is 500" also carries "this is from the rate-limiting section of the API docs." That change alone cut top-20 retrieval failures by 35%; combined with BM25 it reached 49%; and adding a reranking step on top brought the total reduction to 67%. Reranking means over-fetching candidates, then using a more expensive model to re-score and keep only the best few before they go in the window.

Step back and see the shape of the day. Each technique — recursive splitting, overlap, the right embedding dimensions, an ANN index, hybrid search, contextual prefixes, reranking — is a lever on the same goal: get the right small slice of a giant corpus into the window beside the question. That's RAG. It is the bridge between last week's "the model can only use what's in the window" and the rest of the program, where you'll build, measure, and harden these pipelines at a client. Tomorrow we go deeper into making retrieval actually accurate; today you've built the map of the whole pipeline.

Deep dive: the numbers behind the pipeline

Optional. ~10 minutes. The math and the knobs.

When NOT to build RAG. If your whole corpus fits the window — Anthropic's rule of thumb is under ~200,000 tokens, roughly 500 pages — skip retrieval and paste it all in. RAG and chunking earn their complexity only once the corpus outgrows the window. Always size the corpus before you build a pipeline.

The pgvector index, decoded. CREATE INDEX ON items USING hnsw (embedding vector_l2_ops) WITH (m = 16, ef_construction = 64); — HNSW is the ANN index type (better speed/recall than IVFFlat, no training step, buildable on an empty table); m and ef_construction trade build time and memory for recall. Match the operator class to your distance: <-> L2, <=> cosine, <#> negative inner product. With no index, pgvector does exact (slow) search.

Worked example — chunk + overlap math
  • Chunk size: 500 tokens
  • Overlap at 15%: ~75 tokens repeated per boundary
  • A 50,000-token doc → ~118 chunks (vs ~100 with no overlap)
  • Each chunk embedded once with text-embedding-3-small (1536-dim)

The overlap costs ~18% more chunks (and storage) but rescues every fact that straddles a boundary. Drop overlap to 0 and you save storage but reopen the seam problem; push it to 40% and you're paying to store the same sentences three times. 10–20% is the sweet spot for a reason.

Dimensions are a dial. A 256-dim text-embedding-3-large vector still beats full 1536-dim ada-002 on MTEB. Shortening vectors (Matryoshka) cuts storage and comparison cost across millions of chunks — often a better lever than chasing the highest-dimension model. Just never mix embedding models between ingestion and query: different models, incompatible spaces.

Contextual Retrieval, in one line. Prepend chunk-specific context before embedding + BM25 indexing → top-20 failures down 35%; with BM25 hybrid → 49%; add reranking → 67%. Cheap technique, large effect.
