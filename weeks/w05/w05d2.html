Requirements Synthesis: Discovery to Spec

Week 5 · Day 2 Client Discovery & Spec Craft ~18 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

A stakeholder says "the system should be fast and easy to use," and everyone in the room nods. Six weeks later the demo lands wrong, and the postmortem discovers that "fast" meant three different things to three different people. Today you learn the craft that prevents that: running discovery conversations that surface what stakeholders actually mean, hunting the ambiguity in what they said, and compressing it into a spec precise enough that a teammate — or an AI agent — can build from it without guessing. Scroll the reading; the panel on the right turns one vague stakeholder sentence into a precise, testable spec, one refinement at a time.

The whole game

Ambiguity in, garbage out — now with proof

Requirements have been written in natural language since software began, and natural language is inherently imprecise. The official EARS guide states the failure chain plainly: requirements problems propagate to lower levels, creating "unnecessary volatility and risk, impacting programme schedule and cost." A fuzzy sentence at the top becomes a wrong design in the middle and a rebuilt feature at the bottom. That has always been true — but it used to be arguable, the kind of thing process people said and engineers shrugged at.

It is no longer arguable, because we can now measure it. A 2025 study called SpecFix looked at what happens when you hand ambiguous problem descriptions to LLM-based code generation: "unclear problem descriptions may lead to incorrect program generation." The team built a tool that repairs the ambiguity in the description before any code is generated — and on the benchmark problems it modified (43.58% of them needed repair), Pass@1 jumped by 30.9%. Same model, same task, same effort; the only variable was how precisely the requirement was stated. Even better, the repairs transferred: a description clarified for one model made other models more accurate too (+10.48%).

Sit with what that means for your job. In the AI era, spec precision is a code-quality lever, not a documentation nicety. Every ambiguity you fail to surface in a discovery conversation gets resolved anyway — later, silently, by whoever or whatever implements the spec, by guessing. Today's craft is the pipeline that stops the guess: discovery conversation → ambiguity hunt → precise requirement → testable acceptance criteria. Each stage exists to squeeze interpretation out of the words before the words become code.

Stage 1 · discovery

Interview stakeholders about success, not features

Discovery starts with a stakeholder interview — in Nielsen Norman Group's definition, "a conversation with a person who has a vested interest in a project with a goal of gathering insights to drive the project's success." Note what that definition does not say: it doesn't say "a meeting where the client lists features." The beginner walks in with a notepad titled what should it do? The practitioner walks in with a different first question: what does success look like?

NN/g's recommended coverage gives you a four-area checklist to walk in every discovery conversation: success metrics (how will this person know the project worked?), priorities (what matters most, what's negotiable?), history and expertise (what's been tried, what does this person know that you don't?), and process and workflow (how does the work actually flow today?). Features fall out of those four areas naturally — and features gathered without them are exactly the ones that get rebuilt, because nobody asked what problem they were supposed to solve.

Coverage matters more than cleverness. A merely competent interviewer who systematically touches all four areas will out-discover a brilliant one who improvises, because the expensive failures are the areas nobody visited at all. The findings, NN/g notes, "should help inform all aspects of your project: UX research, ideation, priorities, timeline, resources, and stakeholder-engagement plans" — which is a polite way of saying the interview is the foundation everything else stands on. For an FDE, whose whole role is landing at a client and figuring out what to build, this is not a soft skill on the side of the engineering. It is the first engineering artifact of the engagement.

Stage 1 · probing

Follow-up questions are where discovery actually happens

The first answer a stakeholder gives you is almost never the real one — it's the rehearsed one. The real material comes out under open-ended probing: "Tell me more about that." "Can you give me an example?" "Why is that important to you?" Each of those forces the stakeholder off the script and into the specifics where requirements actually live. "We need better reporting" is a script line; "last quarter our VP spent two days hand-building a spreadsheet because the export drops the region column" is a requirement.

The failure modes are just as well-catalogued as the techniques: interviewing too late (after the decisions the interview should have informed), asking closed-ended questions that yield a yes/no where a story was available, and interrupting — filling the silence the stakeholder was about to fill with the thing you needed to hear. Notice that all three are coverage failures, not intelligence failures. You don't miss requirements because you aren't clever; you miss them because of "lack of familiarity with the domain, excessive cognitive load, and information overload" — the three pressures a CMU study identifies as the reason human interviewers drop threads mid-conversation.

That same study (Shen, Singhal & Breaux, 2025) has a finding you should carry into every engagement: GPT-4o-generated follow-up questions were rated no worse than human-authored ones for clarity, relevancy, and informativeness — and when the generation was guided by a taxonomy of common interviewer mistakes, the LLM's questions beat the humans'. Read that carefully, because the lesson isn't "let AI run your interviews." It's that a mistake-and-coverage framework beats freeform "ask good questions" — for the model and for you. Walk in with the checklist, track what you haven't covered, and use an AI copilot to catch the threads you dropped while you were busy listening.

Stage 2 · the gate

Hunt the ambiguity before it hunts you

Between the conversation and the spec sits the day's most important discipline: the ambiguity hunt. Take every sentence you captured and interrogate it — could two reasonable people read this differently? "The dashboard should load quickly." Quickly on what hardware, what network, what data volume? Under 200ms or under 5 seconds? Every one of those unanswered questions is a decision someone downstream will make for the stakeholder, without asking.

The formal bar for when you've squeezed enough ambiguity out comes from ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018, the active international standard for requirements engineering. Its widely-taught characteristics of a well-formed requirement include: necessary (delete it and something breaks), unambiguous (interpretable in only one way), complete (needs no further amplification), singular (one requirement per statement — no smuggled "and"), feasible, verifiable (you can devise a test that proves it's met — enhanced when the requirement is measurable), and traceable (you can point back to who needs it and why). "Fast and easy to use" fails four of those at once: it's ambiguous, double-barreled, unverifiable, and traceable to nobody in particular.

The modern move — borrowed straight from spec-driven tooling — is to make the ambiguity visible instead of resolving it silently: mark the spec with an explicit [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] tag wherever a question is still open, and take the list back to the stakeholder as a batch. That tag is a small piece of genius. It converts ambiguity from an invisible landmine into a tracked work item, and it makes the honest state of the spec legible: not "done," but "done except these six named questions." An FDE who returns from discovery with six sharp [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] items looks far more competent than one who returns with false confidence — and ships far fewer rebuilds.

Stage 3 · EARS

EARS: five sentence shapes that remove interpretation

Once you know what the stakeholder means, you need a way to write it down that can't drift back into fuzz. That's EARS — the Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (Mavin et al., 2009). Its generic structure is one line: "While <optional pre-condition>, when <optional trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>." Clauses always appear in the same temporal order — precondition, then trigger, then system, then response — and that fixed order is the anti-ambiguity mechanism. There's exactly one place for each piece of information, so there's exactly one way to read the sentence.

From that skeleton come five patterns, and choosing between them is itself a diagnostic act. Ubiquitous — "The system shall <response>" — for properties that always hold. Event-driven — "When <trigger>, the system shall…" — for responses to things that happen. State-driven — "While <precondition>, the system shall…" — for behavior that holds during a state. Unwanted behaviour — "If <trigger>, then the system shall…" — for handling errors and failures. Optional feature — "Where <feature is included>, the system shall…" — for configurable capability. Complex requirements combine clauses, in the same fixed order. Forcing yourself to pick a pattern forces the question the stakeholder never answered: is this always true, true when something happens, or true while some state holds? Those are different systems.

If this sounds like aerospace ceremony, look at the adopters list — Airbus, Bosch, NASA, Rolls-Royce — and then look at how the official guide sells it: "lightweight, there is little training overhead, no specialist tool is necessary," and notably effective for authors whose first language isn't English. Then look at your own AI tooling: AWS's Kiro writes its requirements.md in EARS — "WHEN a user submits a form with invalid data THE SYSTEM SHALL display validation errors next to the relevant fields." A 2009 notation from jet-engine control systems is now the native language of agentic coding specs, for exactly the reason SpecFix measured: constrained syntax in, better code out.

Stage 3 · force

MUST, SHOULD, MAY — and the discipline of not saying MUST

EARS gives a requirement its shape; RFC 2119 gives it its force. Published in 1997 and still the normative-language baseline for specs everywhere, it defines the capitalized keywords you've seen in every standard you've ever read. MUST (equivalently SHALL) means "an absolute requirement of the specification" — no judgment call, no context where skipping it is okay. SHOULD means "there may exist valid reasons in particular circumstances to ignore a particular item, but the full implications must be understood and carefully weighed" — a strong default with a documented escape hatch. MAY means "truly optional" — an implementation is conformant with or without it.

Three words, three completely different contracts — and stakeholder language flattens all three into "should." When a client says "it should log every transaction," your job is to ask the RFC 2119 question out loud: if a transaction ever goes unlogged, is that a compliance incident (MUST), a quality issue we'd accept under load (SHOULD), or a nice-to-have (MAY)? The answer changes the architecture. MUST-log means durable write-ahead logging in the transaction path; SHOULD-log might be an async best-effort queue. Same stakeholder sentence, two different systems, and only the keyword forces the decision to be made consciously.

The RFC carries one more instruction that most people skip, and it's the most FDE-relevant line in the document: imperatives "MUST only be used where it is actually required for interoperation or to limit behavior which has potential for causing harm" — not to impose your implementation preferences. A spec where everything is MUST is a spec where nothing is: it strips the implementer of every degree of freedom, including the ones you didn't actually care about, and it hides the three requirements that genuinely are load-bearing inside forty that aren't. Spending MUSTs sparingly is the same budget discipline you learned about tokens in Week 1 — applied to authority instead of context.

Stage 4 · Gherkin

Acceptance criteria: the rule, instantiated as examples

An EARS requirement states the rule. But rules are still abstractions, and abstractions still leave room to argue about edge cases. The final compression stage makes the requirement concrete: acceptance criteria written as executable examples, in the Gherkin syntax that Cucumber made standard. Given describes the initial context — "the scene of the scenario… typically something that happened in the past." When is the event or action — and the docs warn you to keep implementation details out of it (the user submits the form, not "the user POSTs to /api/v2/forms"). Then is "an expected outcome, or result," verified by an observable assertion — something you can actually see or measure, not an internal state you hope is right.

The syntax scales with structure, not verbosity. And / But chain steps within a section. A Background extracts the Givens shared by every scenario in the file, so you write the setup once. A Scenario Outline with an Examples table parameterizes one scenario over many input rows — one shape, a dozen concrete cases. And every file "must always" open with a Feature: line and a short description, so the examples stay anchored to the requirement they serve. The craft bar: a good scenario is a concrete case of a business rule in three to five steps. Longer than that, and you're describing a workflow, not testing a rule.

Here's the bridge that ties the whole day together: EARS and Gherkin are the same requirement at two altitudes. EARS states the rule — "When a user submits a form with invalid data, the system shall display validation errors next to the relevant fields." Gherkin instantiates it — Given a registration form, When the user submits it with an empty email field, Then an "email is required" error appears next to the email input. If you can't write the Gherkin, your EARS statement wasn't verifiable. If you can't state the EARS rule, your scenarios are just anecdotes. Writing both, and checking each against the other, is the cheapest requirements review you will ever run.

Stage 5 · productized

Spec-driven development: the pipeline, encoded in tooling

Everything you just learned as human craft has now been productized as spec-driven development, and you should recognize the shape instantly. GitHub's Spec Kit (v0.12.3) walks a fixed artifact chain: /speckit.constitution establishes the project's governing principles, /speckit.specify defines what you're building — requirements and user stories — then /speckit.clarify, then /speckit.plan, /speckit.tasks, and /speckit.implement, with /speckit.analyze checking cross-artifact consistency. Each phase emits a Markdown artifact that feeds the next — the same discovery→spec→plan funnel you'd run at a client, encoded as slash commands. AWS's Kiro does the same with a three-file chain: requirements.md (EARS, as you saw) → design.md → tasks.md.

The detail worth studying is where the tools put the gate. Spec Kit's docs mandate running "the structured clarification workflow before creating a technical plan to reduce rework downstream" — /speckit.clarify performs sequential, coverage-based questioning and records the answers, and specs carry explicit [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] markers rather than silent assumptions. Clarify-before-plan, coverage-based questioning, ambiguity made visible instead of guessed away: the toolmakers independently converged on exactly the discipline this reading has been teaching, because the cost curve forced them to. Agents amplify whatever you feed them — precise spec in, working code out; ambiguity in, confidently wrong code out, at machine speed.

Which hands you the day's closing argument. The same gate applies to human stakeholders. An ambiguity you don't surface in the discovery conversation becomes a [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] that an agent — or a teammate — resolves by guessing. The FDE's edge isn't typing speed or model access; everyone at the client has those now. The edge is standing at the top of the funnel, running the interview that finds what success means, hunting the ambiguity while it's still cheap, and writing the EARS-and-Gherkin spec that makes everything downstream — human and AI alike — measurably better. That is requirements synthesis: discovery in, spec out, guessing eliminated.

FDE mindset

The most expensive line in any project is the one two people read differently and neither noticed. You are the person paid to notice. Interview for success, hunt the ambiguity, write it so it can only be read one way, and prove it with an example. Everything downstream — the plan, the tasks, the code, the agent runs — inherits the precision you established here.

Deep dive: one vague sentence, worked end-to-end

Optional. ~10 minutes. The full pipeline applied to a single stakeholder quote.

The raw quote. A payments-ops lead says in discovery: "When something goes wrong with a payment, the team should know about it fast." Nod-worthy, and useless as written. Run the pipeline.

1 · Probe. "Can you give me an example?" surfaces the real event: last month a card processor outage produced 4,000 failed charges and the team found out from angry customers, not from the system. "Why is that important?" surfaces the success metric: ops wants to open an incident before customers notice. So "something goes wrong" means payment failure rate exceeds a threshold, "the team" means the on-call ops engineer, and "fast" means before the support queue does.

2 · Ambiguity hunt (29148 bar). The original sentence is ambiguous (what is "wrong"? "fast"?), non-singular ("know about it" bundles detection + notification), and unverifiable (no test can prove "fast"). Threshold and window are still open → mark them: [NEEDS CLARIFICATION: failure-rate threshold and measurement window — proposed 5% over 5 minutes, confirm with ops lead].

3 · EARS (unwanted-behaviour pattern) + RFC 2119 force

"If the payment failure rate exceeds 5% over any 5-minute window, then the monitoring system shall page the on-call ops engineer within 60 seconds." — one trigger, one system, one measurable response. Force check: unlogged pages are an incident-response failure, so this is a MUST/SHALL. A companion "the system SHOULD include the three most recent failure reasons in the page" is genuinely a SHOULD — valid reasons to drop it under load exist and are understood.

4 · Gherkin (the rule, instantiated)

Given 100 payment attempts in the last 5 minutes
And 6 of them failed
When the monitoring window closes
Then the on-call ops engineer receives a page within 60 seconds.

Add a Scenario Outline with an Examples table for 4%-no-page / 5%-boundary / 6%-page rows, and the threshold ambiguity can never silently regress.

Why bother, in numbers. SpecFix's result is the cost-benefit in one line: repairing ambiguity in 43.58% of problem descriptions raised generated-code Pass@1 by 30.9% — and the repaired description helped other models too (+10.48%). Ten minutes of synthesis buys correctness for every implementer downstream, human or agent. That ratio is why this is Week 5's core craft.
