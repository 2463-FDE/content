Requirements Synthesis: Reframe, Then Specify

Week 5 · Day 2 Problem Scoping & Spec Craft ~20 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

Your client this week says "just fix the calendar" — and the artifacts they attached show a retried booking request creating a second confirmed row. The ask and the evidence disagree, and nobody in the room has noticed. Today is the craft for that gap: following the handover to the real defect, naming it in the client's own currency, and only then compressing it into a spec precise enough that a teammate — or an agent — can build from it without guessing. Two failures to defend against, in order: the requirement everyone read differently, and the one everyone understood perfectly that described the wrong system. Scroll the reading; the panel on the right walks a real client ask down to a testable spec, one rung at a time.

The whole game

The ask is evidence, not instructions

Open your client packet for this week. If you're on Riverbend, Dr. Okonkwo writes: "Our scheduling keeps double-booking the same slot and it's getting embarrassing — two patients show up for one appointment. The team's pretty sure it's a glitch in the calendar widget, so could you just fix the calendar so it stops doing that?" If you're on Meridian, it's "Customers want to pay online — card and ACH. Let's just add a payment form so they can self-serve. We've had a few 'I was charged twice' complaints, but I think people are just confused." Both are friendly, specific, and actionable. Both name the fix. Everyone nods.

Now open what came attached. Riverbend's export has two rows for the same slot id, both marked confirmed, created 400 milliseconds apart for different patients. The server log shows a booking request that took 2.11 seconds, then a second request for the same slot — both returned success. The booking handler checks whether the slot is taken and then inserts, with no transaction around the pair, and the table definition has no uniqueness constraint on the slot. Meridian's pile is the same story in a different currency: one retried payment request, two rows.

Nothing in either pile is a calendar bug or a missing form. The artifacts describe a retry that produced a duplicate, and the client has already told you the cause. Here is the day's whole game: the ask is data about what the client believes, and the handover is data about what is true. When they disagree, the spec follows the evidence — and your first job is to notice they disagree at all.

The reframe

The XY problem — and why a perfect spec can still be wrong

This pattern has a name older than any of the tooling: the XY problem. The customer needs a solution to X, forms a theory about the cause, and asks you for Y — the fix their theory implies. You get handed Y. "Just fix the calendar" is Y. "Two patients stop showing up for one slot" is X. The single most important question in the FDE's vocabulary exists to get from one to the other: "What are you trying to accomplish?"

Notice how badly this fails silently. You can fix the calendar widget. It will demo. The double-books keep happening, because the race was never in the UI — and now the client has paid for a fix and lost trust in the diagnosis. The canonical FDE framing is blunt: building Y instead of X is the most expensive mistake in the role, and its usual product is shelfware — software that works, ships, and is never adopted, because it addressed a problem nobody had.

Which sets up the distinction that governs the rest of today, and that most requirements training misses. Ambiguous and wrong are different failures. Everything you'll learn in the back half of this reading — the 29148 well-formedness bar, EARS, RFC 2119, Gherkin — defends against a requirement that two people can read two ways. None of it defends against a requirement that is perfectly clear and describes the wrong system. A flawlessly unambiguous, fully verifiable, EARS-shaped spec for the calendar widget is the most expensive artifact you could produce this week. Reframe first; then specify. And when you push back, push back with an alternative, not a no — "not the calendar, and here's the smaller thing that actually stops it" is a proposal; "that's not the problem" is an argument.

Stage 1 · dig

Dig where the evidence is, on a coverage checklist

You reframe by asking, not by asserting. Nielsen Norman Group defines a stakeholder interview as "a conversation with a person who has a vested interest in a project with a goal of gathering insights to drive the project's success" — note what that does not say: it isn't a meeting where the client lists features. The beginner asks what should it do? The practitioner asks what does success look like? — and, on brownfield, what do the artifacts say is actually happening?

NN/g's four coverage areas give you the checklist: success metrics (how will this person know it worked?), priorities (what's negotiable?), history and expertise (what's been tried, what do they know that you don't?), and process and workflow (how does the work actually flow today?). Your packet's dig list is those four areas pointed at this week's evidence: Are the two confirmed rows a UI glitch, or two successful inserts — and what created the second? Walk read-check-then-insert under two near-simultaneous requests: what stops both from passing the check? If the client retries a slow booking POST, what makes the server treat it as the same booking? Each is open-ended, each is answerable from the handover, and together they walk the client from their theory to yours without ever telling them they were wrong.

Coverage beats cleverness, and the research says so twice. Interviewers drop threads not from lack of intelligence but from "lack of familiarity with the domain, excessive cognitive load, and information overload." And in a 2025 CMU study, GPT-4o's follow-up questions were rated no worse than human-authored ones — then beat them once generation was guided by a taxonomy of common interviewer mistakes. The lesson isn't "let AI run your interviews." It's that a mistake-and-coverage framework outperforms freeform "ask good questions," for the model and for you.

Stage 2 · name it

Name the real problem in the client's own currency

The dig gets you to the technical truth: booking does a read-check-then-insert with no transaction and no unique constraint, and the client retries slow requests, so two inserts pass the same check. That sentence is correct, and it is useless in the room. Dr. Okonkwo did not ask for a concurrency-control review; she asked why two patients showed up for one appointment. The reframe only lands when you restate it in the currency she already counts in.

So translate. Double-booking is a correctness defect, not a UI bug — and it costs clinician time that was sold twice, patients who get billed twice, and trust that is expensive to rebuild. On Meridian: storing the PAN and logging the CVV isn't "a security nit," it is a direct PCI-DSS violation whose remedy — tokenize, never store sensitive authentication data — happens to shrink the audit surface rather than grow it. That's the shape of a good reframe: it names the real defect, prices it in the client's terms, and arrives with a smaller, cheaper alternative than the one they asked for.

Write it as one sentence you could say out loud, because that sentence is the first line of your spec: "You asked for a calendar fix; the logs show a retried booking creating a second confirmed row, so what I want to spec is an idempotency key plus a database-level slot constraint — smaller than a calendar rewrite, and it makes the double-book impossible instead of unlikely." That is the artifact this day produces. And it is worth being precise about why it matters: an FDE owns outcomes, not outputs. The merged PR is the output. Two patients no longer showing up for one slot is the outcome — and it is the only one of the two the client will remember.

Stage 3 · the gate

Hunt the ambiguity before it hunts you

You have the right problem now. That does not make it a specifiable one — and this is where the second failure mode starts. Take every sentence in your reframe and interrogate it: could two reasonable people read this differently? "The system should handle retries safely." Safely for how long — is a repeat booking an hour later the same request or a new one? "Two patients show up for one slot" — is the constraint one appointment per slot, or one per slot per provider? Every unanswered question there is a decision someone downstream will make on the client's behalf, without asking.

The formal bar comes from ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018, the active requirements-engineering standard. Its widely-taught characteristics of a well-formed requirement: necessary (delete it and something breaks), unambiguous (interpretable in only one way), complete, singular (no smuggled "and"), feasible, verifiable (you can devise a test that proves it — enhanced when measurable), and traceable (back to who needs it and why). "Fast and easy to use" fails four at once.

The modern move — borrowed from spec-driven tooling — is to make ambiguity visible rather than resolve it silently: mark the spec with an explicit [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] tag wherever a question is open, and take the list back as a batch. That tag converts ambiguity from an invisible landmine into a tracked work item, and makes the honest state of the spec legible: not "done," but "done except these six named questions." An FDE who returns with six sharp open questions looks far more competent than one who returns with false confidence — and ships far fewer rebuilds.

And unlike most process advice, this one is measured. The 2025 SpecFix study repaired ambiguous problem descriptions before handing them to an LLM for code generation: on the benchmark problems that needed repair (43.58% of them), Pass@1 rose 30.9% — same model, same task, same effort. The repairs even transferred across models (+10.48%). Spec precision is a code-quality lever, not a documentation nicety. Every ambiguity you don't surface gets resolved anyway — silently, by guessing, at machine speed.

Stage 4 · EARS

EARS: five sentence shapes that remove interpretation

Once you know what the stakeholder means, you need a way to write it down that can't drift back into fuzz. That's EARS — the Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (Mavin et al., 2009). Its generic structure is one line: "While <optional pre-condition>, when <optional trigger>, the <system name> shall <system response>." Clauses always appear in the same temporal order — precondition, then trigger, then system, then response — and that fixed order is the anti-ambiguity mechanism. There's exactly one place for each piece of information, so there's exactly one way to read the sentence.

From that skeleton come five patterns, and choosing between them is itself a diagnostic act. Ubiquitous — "The system shall <response>" — for properties that always hold. Event-driven — "When <trigger>, the system shall…" — for responses to things that happen. State-driven — "While <precondition>, the system shall…" — for behavior that holds during a state. Unwanted behaviour — "If <trigger>, then the system shall…" — for handling errors and failures. Optional feature — "Where <feature is included>, the system shall…" — for configurable capability. Complex requirements combine clauses, in the same fixed order. Forcing yourself to pick a pattern forces the question the stakeholder never answered: is this always true, true when something happens, or true while some state holds? Those are different systems.

If this sounds like aerospace ceremony, look at the adopters — Airbus, Bosch, NASA, Rolls-Royce — and at how the official guide sells it: "lightweight, there is little training overhead, no specialist tool is necessary," and notably effective for authors whose first language isn't English. Then look at your own tooling: AWS's Kiro writes its requirements.md in EARS. A 2009 notation from jet-engine control systems is now the native language of agentic coding specs, for exactly the reason SpecFix measured: constrained syntax in, better code out.

Stage 4 · force

MUST, SHOULD, MAY — and the discipline of not saying MUST

EARS gives a requirement its shape; RFC 2119 gives it its force. Published in 1997 and still the normative-language baseline for specs everywhere, it defines the capitalized keywords you've seen in every standard you've ever read. MUST (equivalently SHALL) means "an absolute requirement of the specification" — no judgment call, no context where skipping it is okay. SHOULD means "there may exist valid reasons in particular circumstances to ignore a particular item, but the full implications must be understood and carefully weighed" — a strong default with a documented escape hatch. MAY means "truly optional" — an implementation is conformant with or without it.

Three words, three different contracts — and stakeholder language flattens all three into "should." When your client says the system "should never double-book," ask the RFC 2119 question out loud: if one slips through, is that a patient-safety and billing incident (MUST), or something we'd tolerate under load (SHOULD)? The answer changes the architecture. MUST means a database constraint in the write path; SHOULD might be an application-level check. Same sentence, two different systems, and only the keyword forces the decision to be made consciously.

The RFC carries one more instruction that most people skip, and it's the most FDE-relevant line in the document: imperatives "MUST only be used where it is actually required for interoperation or to limit behavior which has potential for causing harm" — not to impose your implementation preferences. A spec where everything is MUST is a spec where nothing is: it strips the implementer of every degree of freedom, including the ones you didn't actually care about, and it hides the three requirements that genuinely are load-bearing inside forty that aren't. Spending MUSTs sparingly is the same budget discipline you learned about tokens in Week 1 — applied to authority instead of context.

Stage 5 · Gherkin

Acceptance criteria: the rule, instantiated as examples

An EARS requirement states the rule, and rules still leave room to argue about edge cases. The final compression stage makes it concrete: acceptance criteria as executable examples, in Cucumber's Gherkin syntax. Given is the initial context — "the scene of the scenario… typically something that happened in the past." When is the event, with implementation details kept out (the user submits the form, not "POSTs to /api/v2/forms"). Then is "an expected outcome, or result," verified by an observable assertion — not an internal state you hope is right.

The syntax scales with structure, not verbosity. Background extracts the Givens shared across a file; a Scenario Outline with an Examples table parameterizes one scenario over many rows. The craft bar: a good scenario is a concrete case of a business rule in three to five steps. Longer, and you're describing a workflow, not testing a rule.

EARS and Gherkin are the same requirement at two altitudes — and your packet tells you which examples to write, asking for test vectors for the retry and concurrent-booking cases. Given a booking for slot 88231 with idempotency key K, When the identical request arrives again, Then the response is the original confirmation and no second row exists. And: Given two bookings for slot 88231 arriving 400 ms apart, When both are processed, Then exactly one succeeds. Write those and the double-book cannot silently regress. If you can't write the Gherkin, your EARS rule wasn't verifiable; if you can't state the rule, your scenarios are anecdotes. Checking each against the other is the cheapest requirements review you will ever run.

Tomorrow you'll meet the tools that encode this chain — Spec Kit's specify-then-clarify-then-plan sequence, Kiro's requirements-design-tasks trio — enforcing the same gate this reading does: clarify before plan. But notice the one thing none of them does for you. A clarification command finds the ambiguities in the spec you wrote. It will never tell you the spec is about a calendar widget when the defect is a race condition in the booking handler. Reframing stays yours. Everyone at the client has the tooling now; the edge is being the person who notices that the ask and the evidence disagree, and says so with a smaller proposal in hand.

FDE mindset

The two most expensive sentences on an engagement are the one two people read differently and neither noticed, and the one everybody understood perfectly that described the wrong system. You are the person paid to catch both — in that order. Follow the evidence to the real problem, name it in the client's currency, write it so it can only be read one way, and prove it with an example. Everything downstream — the plan, the tasks, the code, the agent runs — inherits whichever problem you chose here.

Deep dive: "just fix the calendar," worked end-to-end

Optional. ~10 minutes. The full ladder applied to this week's client ask.

The ask. "Our scheduling keeps double-booking the same slot… the team's pretty sure it's a glitch in the calendar widget, so could you just fix the calendar?" Friendly, specific, and it names the fix. Run the ladder.

1 · Reframe (XY). The attached export has two confirmed rows for the same slot, 400 ms apart, different patients. The server log shows a 2.11-second booking request followed by a second request for that slot, both succeeding. The handler checks whether the slot is taken and then inserts, with no transaction and no uniqueness constraint behind it. So Y is "the calendar widget renders a stale slot"; X is "a retried request created a second booking, and two concurrent requests can both pass the check." Nothing in the evidence points at the UI.

2 · Name it in their currency. Not "a concurrency defect in the booking path" — that sentence is correct and useless in the room. Say: double-books sell the same clinician hour twice, bill two patients for one appointment, and cost more trust than they cost money. Then the proposal, deliberately smaller than the ask: an idempotency key on the booking POST plus a database-level constraint on the slot.

3 · Ambiguity hunt (29148 bar). "Stop double-booking" is non-singular (dedupe on retry and prevent the race are two requirements) and unverifiable as stated. Two questions stay open → [NEEDS CLARIFICATION: is the uniqueness constraint one appointment per slot, or one per slot per provider?] and [NEEDS CLARIFICATION: idempotency-key retention window — proposed 24h, confirm with front desk].

4 · EARS (two requirements, not one) + RFC 2119 force

"If a booking request arrives with an idempotency key that has already been processed, then the scheduling service shall return the original confirmation without creating a new appointment." And: "The scheduling service shall enforce at most one confirmed appointment per slot at the database level." Force check: a double-book is a patient-safety and billing incident, so both are MUST/SHALL — not preferences. A companion "the booking UI SHOULD disable the confirm button after first submit" is genuinely a SHOULD: helpful, and no longer load-bearing once the constraint exists.

5 · Gherkin (the two test vectors the packet asks for)

Given a confirmed booking for slot 88231 with idempotency key K
When the identical request is received again
Then the original confirmation is returned And no second appointment row exists.

Given two booking requests for slot 88231 arriving 400 ms apart
When both are processed
Then exactly one is confirmed And the other receives a slot-unavailable response.

What it cost, and what it bought. The reframe took one careful read of the handover. It replaced a calendar rewrite with two constraints, and it turned an unfalsifiable complaint into two scenarios that can regression-test forever. Compare the alternative: a beautifully specified, perfectly unambiguous calendar fix that demos on Friday and double-books on Monday. SpecFix quantifies only the second half of this — repairing ambiguity in 43.58% of descriptions raised Pass@1 by 30.9% — but the first half, picking the right problem at all, is the half no tool does for you.
