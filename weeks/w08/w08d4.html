Governance, PII and Responsible AI in a Regulated Deployment

Week 8 · Day 4 Security & Governance ~24 min · reading @150 wpm + diagram + check

Days 1–3 gave you attacks and controls. Today is the layer above both: what data crosses out of your trust boundary, under what agreement, and what you may promise about it. Four artifacts do the work — a data-flow map, a vendor-agreement scope memo, a de-identification routine with a test suite, and model documentation. Both W8 client packets ask for wider, and the honest answer is "not as specified." A flat no loses the room; the move you're learning is a refusal carrying a narrower thing that ships and two conditions that widen it.

The boundary

What leaves, and under what agreement

Every regulated AI deployment reduces to one question asked per hop: what data crosses out of the trust boundary, to whom, and under what written agreement. The governance artifact is not a new diagram — NIST's Privacy Framework says a data map "can be overlaid on existing system/product/service design artifacts." It is an annotation on the architecture you drew.

What the annotation carries is specified, not left to taste. Inventory-and-Mapping reads like a column spec: systems (ID.IM-P1), owners and operators including third parties (P2), data actions (P4), purposes (P5), "data elements within the data actions are inventoried" (P6), and the processing environment — "geographic location, internal, cloud, third parties" (P7). ID.DE-P3 closes it: contracts with those parties "are used to implement appropriate measures." Minimum row: source | data elements | destination | operator | region | purpose | agreement covering this hop | retention | evidence link. A row with an empty agreement cell is a finding, not a backlog item.

Why that granularity earns its keep: under HIPAA the API call is the regulated event. "Disclosure means the release, transfer, provision of access to, or divulging in any manner of information outside the entity holding the information" (45 CFR 160.103) — no volume threshold. P6 and P7 turn "we send the encounter to the model" into "we send name, DOB, MRN and notes to vendor X in us-east-1 under no agreement."

Permission

The contract is the permission

A covered entity may disclose PHI to a business associate "if the covered entity obtains satisfactory assurance" that it will safeguard the information — and those assurances "must be documented through a written contract" meeting §164.504(e) (45 CFR 164.502(e)(1)(i), (e)(2)). The permission does not come from TLS, a SOC 2 report, or the vendor's security page. It comes from an executed document — no document, no permission, and the disclosure is impermissible, not a paperwork gap.

Your cloud LLM vendor is inside the definition: a business associate is anyone who, on a covered entity's behalf, "creates, receives, maintains, or transmits" PHI for a regulated function, "including … data analysis, processing or administration" (160.103). Summarization is data analysis — and the definition reaches subcontractors, so the vendor's inference provider inherits it.

The required terms at 164.504(e)(2) are a checklist you can diff a terms-of-service against: permitted uses stated; no further disclosure beyond the contract; safeguards including subpart C for ePHI; breach reporting "as required by § 164.410"; subcontractors bound to "the same restrictions and conditions"; support for access, amendment, and accounting of disclosures. A standard SaaS ToS satisfies none of it — "we have a contract with the vendor" and "we have a BAA" are different findings.

Nor does signing end the duty: knowing of "a pattern of activity or practice … that constituted a material breach" and failing to cure — ultimately by having "terminated the contract" — is itself non-compliance (164.504(e)(1)(ii)). That standing obligation is why the widening conditions carry owners and dates.

Vendor scope

"The vendor is HIPAA-ready" is not a row you can write

The trap that turns a plausible memo into a wrong one: coverage is scoped surface by surface, not company by company. Anthropic's BAA page covers Chat, Projects, Artifacts, Voice, Research and Skills; file creation and code execution only "excluding network access and use of external websites"; MCP connectors, Enterprise Search and Claude in Chrome are "available to use but sending data to 3rd parties via this feature isn't covered."

Coverage requires an affirmative act, not a plan tier: "Standard Claude Enterprise plans do not include BAA coverage without action from a Primary Owner." And it couples to retention both ways — Claude Code CLI is "only covered under the BAA with ZDR enabled," while "Covered Models require 30-day data retention and aren't available with zero data retention (ZDR) enabled." Some model choices and covered surfaces are mutually exclusive. Even the vendor's own pages disagree about web search — so read the coverage table, dated, on the day you write the memo.

Two controls get mistaken for permission. Default-no-training — Anthropic's commercial products, OpenAI's business and API tiers where data "isn't used for training our models" — is a use control. Thirty-day retention with optional ZDR is a retention control. Neither permits disclosure; under 164.502(e) only the executed agreement does. Roles are contractual too — the customer is Controller, the vendor "acts as a 'Processor'." So the row names a feature: "Claude — covered" is false; "Enterprise Chat with HIPAA activated — covered; the MCP connector hop — not covered" is a finding.

De-identification

Two lawful methods, eighteen categories, one knowledge test

The other lever is shrinking the payload, and "de-identify it first" is lawful in exactly two ways. Expert determination (164.514(b)(1)): a qualified person determines the re-identification risk "is very small … by an anticipated recipient" and documents the analysis. Safe Harbor (b)(2): remove the listed identifiers — "of the individual or of relatives, employers, or household members."

The list runs (A)–(R); three carry most of the engineering weight. (B) geography: ZIP clears only at three digits, and only where that unit holds more than 20,000 people — otherwise 000. (C) dates: every element except year goes, ages over 89 collapse into "90 or older." (R): "any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code," a catch-all that swallows internal surrogate keys. The other fifteen are direct identifiers you'd guess — MRN (H), device serials (M).

So count what the contractor's "de-identified analytics export" achieved by dropping the name column: one of eighteen. It still ships ZIP, dates, MRN and device IDs — still PHI. And Safe Harbor carries a second, non-mechanical condition no library clears for you: the entity "does not have actual knowledge that the information could be used … to identify an individual." The engineer who knows the MRN maps to a patient satisfies that trigger personally.

The tempting fix — hash the MRN, keep the join key — fails on its own subsection. A re-identification code is permitted only if it "is not derived from or related to information about the individual" (164.514(c)). A hash is; irreversibility is not the test. Use a random surrogate in a table you never ship.

Evidence

A redactor is a test suite, not a promise

NIST SP 800-188 supplies the epigraph: "not all tools that merely mask personal information provide sufficient functionality for performing de-identification." It classifies Safe Harbor as prescriptive — focused "on the process of de-identification rather than on the specific properties of the de-identified data" — which is good news, because a process is testable. The stakes warrant it: "once de-identified, the dataset is no longer subject to HIPAA privacy, security, and breach notification regulations." A function you wrote moves data out of a regulatory regime. The §4.6.2 validation checklist is your test plan: verify "that no identifying information is unintentionally included in file data or metadata"; "critically evaluat[e] all default assumptions used by software that performs data modification"; and run a motivated intruder test "to see if reasonably competent outside individuals can perform re-identification using publicly available … datasets."

Completion problem — finish the matrix

Three rows filled; write the fourth, then state the release rule.

  • (A) names — "Pt. R. Ortega, spouse M. Ortega" vs. "the patient's mother". Assert: both go — (b)(2)(i) covers relatives.
  • (C) dates — "admitted 3/4/26, DOB 1934-02-11, age 91" vs. "last Tuesday". Assert: year survives, age becomes 90 or older.
  • (B) ZIP — 02139 (>20,000), 59545 (≤20,000). Assert: 021, 000.
  • (H) MRN — your row. What are the positive fixtures, and where do they live?

The MRN row catches people for two reasons the first three only hint at: fixtures must cover format variants inside free text (MRN 00123, mrn-00123, #123) and identifiers living outside a column — a filename, EXIF, a JSON sibling key. Then the release rule: report recall per category; a miss on any direct identifier is release-blocking.

Run the suite offline — validating a redactor with live model calls leaks the data you are testing. And borrow 800-188's governance wrapper: a Disclosure Review Board, "a de-identification standard with measurable performance levels."

Documentation

The model card is a file in the repo

Mitchell et al. proposed that "released models be accompanied by documentation detailing their performance characteristics," including "benchmarked evaluation … across different cultural, demographic, or phenotypic groups … and intersectional groups." Their stated purpose is exactly the lever you need this week: "to clarify the intended use cases of machine learning models and minimize their usage in contexts for which they are not well suited." A written intended-use section is how "not for this" stops being your opinion and becomes the artifact's.

Form matters as much as content. Hugging Face's Hub spec is what makes it an engineering artifact rather than a slide: "model cards are simple Markdown files with additional metadata… You can find a model card as the README.md file in any model repo," with "a YAML section at the top" carrying license, datasets, base_model and friends. It versions with the model and reviews in a PR. Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.) is the data-side companion.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 places it: "Trustworthy AI depends upon accountability. Accountability presupposes transparency" — spanning "design decisions and training data to model training, the structure of the model, its intended use cases, and how and when deployment, post-deployment, or end user decisions were made and by whom." Then the sentence that kills the "make a nice page about how advanced it is" instinct: "A transparent system is not necessarily an accurate, privacy-enhanced, secure, or fair system." Page and card can carry the same facts; only one has an out-of-scope-use section and a commit history.

Currency

When your citation gets withdrawn, walk down a level

Write the finance memo from memory and you will cite CFPB Circulars 2022-03 and 2023-03 as live authority. Both were withdrawn — items 14 and 7 of 90 FR 20084, May 12, 2025 — and "should not be enforced or otherwise relied upon by the Bureau," though "such withdrawal is not necessarily final." Second trap: site state is not legal state. consumerfinance.gov reduced 2023-03 to an "Archived content" stub, while 2022-03 still renders in full with no banner despite sitting in the same list.

The finding survives anyway, because it never depended on the circular. 12 CFR 1002.9(b)(2): the statement of reasons "must be specific and indicate the principal reason(s)," and statements that the applicant "failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient." Comment 9(b)(2)-2: reasons "must relate to and accurately describe the factors actually considered or scored." Comment -4: only scored factors, and "no factor that was a principal reason for adverse action may be excluded from disclosure." A scorer emitting two generic reason strings regardless of its real drivers is non-compliant on the regulation's own terms.

Comment 9(b)(2)-5 then hands you an implementable selection algorithm: take the factors where the applicant's score "fell furthest below the average score … achieved by applicants whose total score was at or slightly above the minimum passing score"; "any other method that produces results substantially similar" is acceptable. Comment -1 caps useful output at four reasons; -8 forces an automatic-denial factor to surface as itself. That is your mapping's acceptance criteria, written by the regulator — and the habit to take away: when the guidance you learned gets withdrawn, walk one level down and check whether the finding survives.

The delivery move

Say no with a date attached

Both W8 packets ask for wider. "Automatic visit summaries for every patient." "Roll the scorer out wider and make a nice page about how advanced it is." Both hide a defect that width multiplies — an unsignaled PHI disclosure per encounter, an ECOA exposure per applicant — and in both, the surface ask is a communication artifact while the real deliverable is a governance one. So refuse with a dated alternative. Five levers narrow the ask: scope (one clinic, one segment), data (de-identified or minimum-necessary payload), autonomy (draft-for-review), surface (only features the agreement covers), volume (a capped pilot). Each lever traces back to a row in the data map — which is what makes the narrowing arguable rather than timid.

Then two widening conditions, each with an owner, an artifact, and a date. Good conditions are binary and checkable by someone who is not you: "executed BAA covering the surfaces named in the vendor's coverage table, countersigned, on file — Legal, Oct 15." Bad conditions are moods: "once legal is comfortable." State the failure branch out loud — if a condition slips, the gate holds. AI RMF GOVERN 6.2 wants "contingency processes … to handle failures or incidents in third-party … AI systems deemed to be high-risk," and a condition producing no artifact cannot be audited. The sentence to be able to say without notes: "We can ship [gated version] by [date]; it widens to [full version] when (1) [condition A] and (2) [condition B]."

FDE mindset

Governance work fails in two directions: say yes and you shipped the disclosure, say no and you blocked the business. Only the third answer survives a board — name the narrower version that ships this quarter, and the two dated, evidence-backed conditions that widen it. Everything else today exists to supply the evidence they point at.

Deep dive: writing the gate

Optional. ~10 minutes. The difference between a condition and a wish.

A condition has three parts and one property. Parts: the artifact that will exist, the owner who produces it, the date it is due. Property: a person who was not in the room can look at the artifact and answer yes or no without asking you. "Executed BAA covering Enterprise Chat and the file-creation surface, countersigned, filed in the contracts repo — Legal (R. Okafor), Oct 15" passes both tests. "Vendor risk sign-off" fails the property: sign-off on what, evidenced how?

Pair a governance condition with an engineering condition. One condition someone else owns (the agreement, the policy sign-off) and one you own (the redactor hits its per-category recall bar on the fixture suite and passes a motivated-intruder pass; or the reason-code mapping is validated against the model's real drivers at a stated threshold). Two conditions that both belong to Legal means you have no skin in the gate, and the board will read it that way.

It widens to all clinics and to unredacted input when (1) an executed BAA naming those surfaces is countersigned and on file (Legal, Oct 15) and (2) the redactor reports ≥99.5% recall on every direct-identifier category across the fixture suite and passes a motivated-intruder pass (me, Oct 22). If either slips, the gate holds."

Finance, your turn: the narrow version keeps the scorer on its current portfolio segment and adds the corrected reason mapping. Name the two conditions. One should point at the reason-accuracy validation implied by comment 9(b)(2)-5; the other at the fair-lending monitoring spec, with a named owner and a first report date rather than "ongoing."

The vendor section, in banking. The interagency third-party guidance names the clauses to look for and they transfer directly into the memo: access to your own data "in an appropriate and timely manner"; how data "may be shared with regulators … as part of the supervisory process"; "whether the third party is permitted to resell, assign, or permit access to customer data, or the banking organization's data, metadata, and systems, to other entities"; notification of subcontractors and of "compliance lapses, enforcement actions"; and periodic independent audits "of the third party and its relevant subcontractors," with the right to conduct your own. It also warns against SLAs that "incentivize imprudent performance or behavior."

Currency discipline for the whole package. Every citation in the memo carries a retrieval date. Guidance-level sources (circulars, interagency guidance, framework playbooks) get a re-verification date in the gate itself; statute and regulation get a version stamp. You saw two failure modes today: a withdrawn circular that still renders in full, and a vendor's two pages disagreeing about web search. Both are caught by the same habit — quote the source you actually opened, and write down when you opened it.

Production code: the boundary gate

De-identification stops being a promise the moment it is a function with a refusal branch: scrub, re-scan, and raise rather than send.

# Nothing crosses the trust boundary without a Safe Harbor scrub AND an independent re-scan.
# Auth: Bedrock API key in AWS_BEARER_TOKEN_BEDROCK (boto3 reads it; no IAM role).
import re, boto3
bedrock = boto3.client("bedrock-runtime", region_name="us-east-1")

class BoundaryViolation(RuntimeError):
    """Residual identifier post-scrub -- sending it would be a disclosure."""

# 45 CFR 164.514(b)(2)(i) -- 4 of the 18 categories; the fixture suite covers the rest.
SAFE_HARBOR = {
    "H_mrn":   re.compile(r"\b(?:MRN|mrn)[-#\s]?\d{4,10}\b"),
    "G_ssn":   re.compile(r"\b\d{3}-\d{2}-\d{4}\b"),
    "F_email": re.compile(r"\b[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+\b"),
    "C_date":  re.compile(r"\b\d{1,2}[/-]\d{1,2}[/-]\d{2,4}\b"),
}
# Deliberately NOT the same patterns: the gate must fail on what the scrub missed.
RESIDUAL = re.compile(r"\d{4,}|@|\b\d{1,2}[/-]\d{1,2}\b")

def scrub(text: str) -> str:
    for tag, pat in SAFE_HARBOR.items():
        text = pat.sub(f"[{tag}]", text)
    return text

def summarize(note: str) -> str:
    payload = scrub(note)
    hit = RESIDUAL.search(payload)
    if hit:                        # refuse; never "send anyway and log a warning"
        raise BoundaryViolation(f"residual {hit.group()!r} at {hit.start()}: not sent")
    resp = bedrock.converse(
        modelId="us.anthropic.claude-haiku-4-5-20251001-v1:0",
        system=[{"text": "Summarize the visit. Never invent identifiers."}],
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [{"text": payload}]}],
        inferenceConfig={"maxTokens": 400, "temperature": 0},
    )
    return resp["output"]["message"]["content"][0]["text"]

Four regexes are not Safe Harbor — they are 4 of 18, and 800-188 warns that tools which "merely mask" are not de-identification. Two things make this production-shaped: the residual check is independent of the scrub patterns (re-scanning with the same regexes always passes, which proves nothing), and the failure branch is raise, not a warning. The fixture suite is what proves the recall behind it.
