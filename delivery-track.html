Weeks 7–10 · client engagement

The Delivery Track

One page, given to you before you do anything, because a rubric you cannot see is just a vague instruction wearing a lab coat. Every Monday you present to the client and are scored against what is written here. The trend is what matters, not any single Monday.

Set your week below and the page grows with the engagement — Week 7 shows the starting layer of every section, and each week adds the next one. Nothing is hidden from you; "show every week" opens the whole thing whenever you want to read ahead.

Your week
The contract

Reachable and numbered

Everything you present is reachable and numbered. There are two ways to get there — and one way that is not a delivery at all.

Reachable
A person can click to it in the product.
Terminal output is not a demo. If you say something is ready, the client expects to click it.
Numbered
One measurement that proves the change — taken before and after.
"More performant" without a measurement is an opinion in an engineer's voice.
Path A — shipped
On main
Merged. Not a PR, not a branch, not "ready to merge." This is the default and most things should land here. On demo day there were eight open PRs between the four of you and two entire weeks of work that existed and could not be shown. Unmerged work is worth zero to a client.
Path B — POC + estimate
Clickable ahead of the backend
A working interface running ahead of the backend, plus a blocker card with two numbers, a named owner and a conversion date. Legitimate when the remaining work is genuinely gated — a compliance review, a decision that isn't yours, a dependency you don't own. Not legitimate as a way to avoid finishing.
Neither — not a delivery
A branch, a terminal, a description
To a client these are the same thing: nothing. A POC with no date behind it is "it's in a PR" wearing a new hat. If the same item is Path B twice running without the estimate moving, that gets said out loud.

Path B is a real delivery, and often the smarter one: the client reacts to the interface — the thing they can actually judge — while the backend finishes behind it. Done well the feature lands the day the backend is done, instead of weeks later while the UX is still being argued about.

Whichever path — say the state first, before you show anything.
"This works today. Here is what I need more time to make robust, and here is how long."

Silence reads as either nothing happened or everything is finished. Both are wrong.

Neither

Three failures in one sentence: not on main, not reachable, and a bare comparative with no number. The work may genuinely be excellent. To the client this is indistinguishable from a week where nothing happened — and that is the whole lesson of demo day.

Every Monday

The client steering meeting — 12 minutes, hard stop

All four of you present last week's work to the client. In the same meeting the client hands down updated requirements for the week ahead. That handoff is the forcing function: it is what makes re-speccing necessary, and it is where the backlog comes from. Shorter slots are deliberately harder — five reps each across W7–W10.

The 12 minutes, said out loud 1 / 4
Slot 1 · 2 minthe opening

The outcome, before any method

"Two things landed this week and one didn't.

The one that landed: agents no longer read a whole ticket history to answer a repeat question — the assistant surfaces the three closest past tickets on the same screen. On the 40 questions your team gave us, the right ticket is in the top five 81% of the time, up from 62%. That comes from the eval run, not from my own test.

The one that didn't: posting a reply back into the ticket needs a service account I can't issue myself. I'll come back to that in the last two minutes with a date.

What I'd like from you today: watch whether the three suggestions are the ones your agents would actually have opened."

Why it scores: state before demo, outcome framed as agent time rather than retrieval quality, one number with a baseline and an outside oracle, the gap named up front instead of discovered, and an explicit ask that makes the question slot useful.

Slot 1 · the same week, badly

Method first, state never

"So this week I looked at the retrieval side. I tried a few things — started with plain keyword, that wasn't great, then moved to embeddings, and I ended up with a hybrid approach plus a re-ranker, which seems to work a lot better. Let me just pull it up… one sec… okay, so if I open a ticket here…"

What went wrong in 20 seconds: opens with method, no outcome, no number, no honest state, and dead air while it loads. The client has no idea whether the week was good. "Seems to work a lot better" is the exact sentence the claims audit exists to delete.

Slot 4 · 20 seccalibration

Reporting last week's estimate, out loud

"Quick calibration before I stop. Last Monday I told you the re-index was two days. It took five. The reason: nobody had exported tickets since 2024 and the export job had to be rebuilt first — I didn't know that dependency existed when I gave you the number. The week before I was off by one day, and before that three. The gap is closing but it's still there, so treat my estimates as the middle of a range."

Why this is worth 20 seconds every week: nobody is scored on estimate accuracy — you are scored on whether the gap shrinks and whether you can say why it missed. Saying it before you are asked is what makes the next number believable.

Translation pairs 1 / 4
W2 · retrieval

Hybrid retrieval + re-ranker

Engineer voice
"Swapped naive top-k for hybrid retrieval with a re-ranker; recall@5 went from 0.62 to 0.81 on the 40-question gold set."
Client voice
"When an agent asks a question, the answer they need is in the first five results four times out of five. It used to be three."
The number
recall@5 0.62 → 0.81, 40 questions

The move: "recall@5" becomes "four times out of five," and the baseline survives as "it used to be three." Deleting the number is the most common way this rep gets faked.

W3 · agent reliability

Timeout + circuit breaker

Engineer voice
"Put a 3s timeout and a circuit breaker on the ticket-API call and moved it off the request thread."
Client voice
"When the ticket system has a bad twenty minutes, the assistant goes quiet instead of freezing the whole screen. Your agents keep working."
The number
worst-case page wait 30s → 3s

The move: the client frame is not "reliability," it is "your people can still work during an outage." Same fact, rebuilt from what it costs them.

W1 · cost guard

Token budget + caching

Engineer voice
"Added a per-request token ceiling and cached embeddings, so we re-embed on change instead of per run."
Client voice
"A busy day can't produce a surprise bill any more — there's a hard ceiling per request, and we stopped paying twice for the same document."
The number
cost per 1,000 assists $4.10 → $0.90

The move: "token ceiling" means nothing to a COO; "can't produce a surprise bill" is the same fact in the frame they budget in. Cost translations land harder than latency ones — most clients feel money faster than milliseconds.

W4 · graph / multi-agent

Assembled view across services

Engineer voice
"Replaced 37 per-encounter queries with one graph traversal; assembled-view p95 dropped from 3.8s to 0.9s."
Client voice
"The full customer picture opens in under a second instead of nearly four. That's the screen your team lives on all day."
The number
p95 3.8s → 0.9s

The move: name whose screen and how often. A latency number attached to a job someone does 200 times a day is a business case; the same number on its own is trivia.

Scored every Monday

The five dimensions

Pass = mean ≥ 3.0 with no dimension below 2. Technical accuracy ≤ 1 fails the week regardless of your mean.

Three things you should know about that bar, because you would find out anyway: Week 7 is scored but not passed — it is your baseline, nobody fails W7. The 0–4 descriptions come from what actually happened at demo day; the cut point is a judgment call built for this cohort, so argue with it if you think it is wrong. And this is formative and the trainer's, not a Revature grade — it exists so you get five reps instead of one. Optimising it defeats the point.

▾(a)Technical accuracy Can you explain the mechanism, and its boundary?
0
Says something factually wrong about the mechanism and doesn't correct it. Or can't name what was built.
1
Names the component; the mechanism is vague or borrowed ("it uses AI to check the output"). Jargon substitutes for explanation.
2
Correct mechanism at one level: what goes in, what comes out, where it sits. Can't answer "what happens when it fails."
3
+ one alternative you rejected and why, + one failure mode and what happens then. Every claim traceable to the repo.
4
+ you state the boundary — a class of input it won't catch, or a condition where it degrades — unprompted, without overselling the fix.
Technical accuracy is the one dimension with a floor: ≤ 1 fails the week on its own. Everything else is a trend.
"How does it decide which past tickets to show?" 1 / 4
Scores 0
"It's a neural network, so it's about 95% accurate at understanding the ticket."

Fails the week on its own. The 95% is invented, "understanding" is not what happens, and nothing here is traceable to the repo. A 0 in this dimension is the one score that overrides your mean.

Scores 1
"It uses AI to find the most relevant ones — it understands what the ticket means, not just keywords."

Jargon substituting for explanation. Every word is borrowed from marketing copy. Ask "what does 'understands' do when the ticket is one line?" and the answer runs out immediately.

Scores 3W2
"Every past ticket is turned into a set of numbers that stand in for its meaning, and we look for tickets whose numbers sit closest to the new one. I also tried plain keyword search — it missed anything worded differently, so we run both and merge. When a ticket is one line with no detail, the closest matches are close to random, so it shows nothing rather than three wrong things."

No jargon survived, and the mechanism is still exactly right — plus one rejected alternative and one failure mode with the behaviour named.

Scores 4
"…and the boundary is worth naming: it matches on wording, not on what was actually wrong. Two tickets described the same way with different root causes look identical to it. That's a real class of miss, and I'd rather you hear it from me than find it in month three."

The boundary, unprompted, with no oversold fix. This is the single cheapest way to move from 3 to 4 — and the thing most engineers withhold because it feels like admitting weakness. It reads as competence.

▾(b)Audience adaptation Built from their frame, not yours slowed down.
0
Unglossed jargon at a non-technical listener (p95, idempotent, HNSW). Or condescension.
1
The same explanation you'd give an engineer, delivered slower. Simplification by volume, not by frame.
2
Reframes to their world at least once — cost, risk, time, or their job. Some jargon survives.
3
Built from their frame: opens with the outcome that matters to them, then the mechanism. Unavoidable terms glossed in one clause. Ends by checking understanding.
4
+ asks the clarifying question before answering when the ask is ambiguous. Handles a fashionable misconception without making them feel stupid.
Your client is a COO, a VP of lending, a compliance officer. They are smart and they are not engineers. Simplifying by frame means opening with what it costs, what it risks, or what it does to their job — then the mechanism, once they want it.

Simplification by volume, not by frame. Same content, half the speed, plus "if that makes sense" — which asks the client to admit confusion rather than checking it yourself.

Scores 3

Scores 4W5 · XY

The clarifying question, before the answer

Client says: "Can you make it smarter? It should just know the answer." That ask has at least three meanings.

"Before I answer — when you say it should know the answer, do you mean it should draft the reply for the agent to send, find the one past ticket that already solved it, or close simple tickets without an agent? Those are three different builds and one of them I'd argue against. Which is closest to what you pictured?"

The ambiguity is named without making the client feel imprecise, the options are in their language, and a refusal is signalled before a month of work goes the wrong way. This is the XY reframe from W5, live.

Scores 4misconception

Handling a fashionable misconception

Client says: "My nephew says you can just feed the whole ticket database into the AI now. Is all this search stuff even necessary?"

"He's not wrong that the limit moved a lot — that used to be impossible. Two things still bite: it costs per use, so pasting three years of tickets into every question gets expensive fast, and accuracy drops when the useful paragraph is buried in a very large pile. Searching first is what keeps both under control. If the pile ever gets small enough, I'll happily delete my own code."

Concede what's true first. No correction of the nephew, no defensiveness, two concrete reasons, and an explicit willingness to be wrong later. Making a client feel stupid is scored a 0 no matter how right you are.

▾(c)Claim with evidence Number, baseline, and where it came from.
0
Bare comparatives — "faster," "more performant," "much better."
1
One number, unsourced or non-comparable. "It's about 3x."
2
One claim carries a number and a baseline (p95 3.8s → 0.9s); other comparatives stay bare.
3
Every comparative claim has number + baseline + where the number came from. Unmeasured claims are stated as unmeasured, or cut.
4
+ you state a claim the evidence does not support and say so. Cite the artifact path so someone else can re-derive the number.
The oracle rule. Your number needs an oracle that isn't your own code. A test whose expected value mirrors the implementation cannot catch a wrong implementation.
Four claims, scored 1 / 4
Scores 0
"Search is way more accurate now."

Bare comparative. Nothing to check, nothing to dispute, nothing to act on. It is an opinion in an engineer's voice.

Scores 1
"Accuracy is up about 30%."

A number with no baseline and no source. 30% of what, measured how, against when? A number that can't be re-derived is worse than no number — it sounds checkable and isn't.

Scores 3
"On the 40 questions your team wrote, the right ticket is in the top five 81% of the time, up from 62%. That's the eval run in artifacts/eval/2026-08-01.json — the expected answers came from your agents, not from my code."

Number, baseline, source, and an oracle that is not the implementation. The last clause is what makes it evidence rather than a self-graded exam.

Scores 4
"…and I want to be straight about one thing: I think it also saves agents time, but I haven't measured it. Nobody has timed a ticket before and after. Treat that as unproven until we do."

States a claim the evidence does not support, before anyone asks. Said as a bare "agents are faster now," the same belief scores a 0. The identical thought, honestly framed, is the difference between the top and bottom of this dimension.

▾(d)Demo execution On main, reachable, prepared, scripted — including the failure.
0
Nothing running. Terminal presented as a demo. Or demoing work that isn't on main.
1
It runs, but from a terminal or script the client could never reach. Improvised path, dead air.
2
In the product UI, reachable — but unprepared data. Pauses, retries, "normally this works."
3
On main, reachable from the UI, prepared data, a planned sequence of screens, under the time cap. You state up front what is thin vs hardened.
4
+ a failure is handled on-script — you rehearsed what to say when it breaks, and said it without losing the room. Hardening time requested with a duration attached.
Plan the demo as a script: which screens, in what order, against what data, and what you say when something fails. Rehearse the failure. It will happen.

The screen-by-screen plan and the failure script live in the pre-mortem sheet (§3 and §4), each with three filled examples.

Underneath the score

Four habits

Each one is a thing that actually happened on demo day, kept because it worked or because its absence cost someone.

01
Track your client's regulator.
The sharpest exchange of demo day happened because the client had read the news and the engineer had not. Know what moved this month.
02
Audit the handover artifact.
The brief you are given is a claim, not evidence. One of you ran the client's own worked example and found it didn't reproduce. Do that by default.
03
Write down what you refused, and why.
Every one of you declined an ask and was right to. The ones that landed were written down and said out loud. The ones that didn't were only ever in the code.
04
Test data is still a finding.
A card number or a patient record in a repo is a finding regardless of provenance, because nobody reading it can tell it's fake. Remove it and say why.

Pass =

you did it just now, start to finish

If any of the three is false, that is what you say first on Monday, before you show anything: "This works today. Here is what I need more time to make robust, and here is how long."

3 · The failure script

For every R above, write the exact words. Not a plan — the words.

If this breaks
I say (≤8 words, then scope it)
I go to

The shape of a good recovery, in order: ① Name it in eight words or fewer. ② Scope it immediately — "my environment, not the feature" — this is the sentence that decides whether the room loses confidence in the demo or in the work. ③ Be somewhere useful within 60 seconds: a chaptered recording, a second screen, the next capability. Never live debugging. ④ Repair it in writing the same day: one paragraph, what broke, why, and that it is fixed.

Do not apologise more than once. The second apology is when the room starts believing you.

Six rules

  1. Two numbers, never one. A single estimate is a guess wearing a suit. "Three days, or eight if it goes to security review" is something a client can plan around. It also forces you to name what the risk actually is, which is usually the more useful half.
  2. Name a person, not a department. The hardest field and the most valuable one. "Blocked on IT" is a place to hide. "Blocked on Dana, who needs 30 minutes to agree the API scope" is a thing someone can act on — and getting a person who doesn't report to you to act is the job.

  4. File it when it appears, not when you present. A blocker discovered Friday and filed Friday is a status update. The same blocker filed Monday morning is an excuse.
  5. The conversion date is a commitment, and it is checked. Path B items convert in W9–W10. That is what those weeks are for.
  6. Estimates get scored against reality — that's the whole point.

Week 9

Split the work before you put a number on it

By now you have two or three weeks of your own history, and you have probably noticed the same thing everyone notices: the parts you forgot are what blew the estimate, not the part you thought about. The fix is mechanical. Never estimate a whole item. List its parts, put numbers on each, add them up.

The parts people leave out, every time: merge, review, and deploy. An agent will write the code in an afternoon and none of those three get faster because of it.

For each part, three numbers instead of two — best case, likely, bad case. Two useful things fall out. First, naming the best case stops you anchoring on it. Second, the spread between best and bad case is your uncertainty, made visible. If you want the standard formula, it weights the middle:

expected = ( best + 4 × likely + bad ) ÷ 6

You do not have to use the formula. What you must do is write the parts down separately — that alone moves most people's estimates closer to reality than any arithmetic.
