System Blueprint

You are not taking ten separate courses. You are building one production AI delivery system, and each week adds or hardens a piece of it. This page shows the whole machine, then lets you practice reading it the way a forward-deployed engineer does: where does a symptom point first, and which trade-off is being made?

Optional depth — outside the Core 30 required path Solo · Weeks 1–5 → Teams of 2–3 · Weeks 6–10 Provider-neutral — your AWS / GCP lane lives on each lesson

The ten-week build, one line

Weeks 1–5 you build the serving path solo on a synthetic insurance brownfield. In Week 6 you inherit a team project: 2–3 engineers on a synthetic banking/payments system. Weeks 6–10 wrap that system in events, infrastructure, observability, security, and a real handoff.

The operations path — how the system changes and survives

This is the loop you build in Weeks 6–10 around the serving path: one road for change, retained failures, signals that decide, and evidence for every intervention. Everything here runs against a synthetic client environment — no live cloud account.

Diagnosis drills — where do you look first?

A client reports a symptom. Pick the component you would inspect first. Checking answers is optional and local to this page; every drill's full diagnosis path is readable below it with or without JavaScript. 0 of 6 worked through

“Answers keep citing a policy we retired last quarter.”

Generation looks fluent, citations are real documents, just outdated ones.

Full diagnosis path

Start at the vector store: is the retired policy still in the corpus, and did re-ingestion run after the retirement? Then walk forward: did context assembly rank the stale passage above fresher evidence? Only if the evidence was correct and the answer still cited the old policy does this become a generation question.

Practiced in Week 2 (retrieval evaluation) and Week 8 (bad-answer root cause)
“Latency doubled after yesterday’s deploy. Error rate is flat.”

Users wait twice as long; nothing is failing outright.

Full diagnosis path

Open observability and compare span durations for the same route before and after the deploy. The widened hop names the suspect: retrieval, assembly, model call, or guardrails. Then read the deploy's diff through the CI/CD gates record to see what changed in that hop, and only then intervene.

Practiced in Week 8 (cross-system correlation) with Week 7's gate evidence
“Document decisions arrive later and later each hour. The answers are still right.”

Quality is fine; the pipeline just keeps slipping further behind.

Full diagnosis path

Read consumer lag per partition on the event backbone: is one consumer group falling behind evenly, or did a rebalance leave partitions cold? Check whether token-aware admission is deferring work by design. Scale consumers or rebalance only after the lag pattern says which it is, and confirm the retry/DLQ path is not silently absorbing a share of the volume.

Practiced in Week 6 (lag, rebalance, backpressure) and Week 8 (saturation signals)
“An agent called a tool it was never supposed to have.”

The call succeeded. Nothing crashed. That is the problem.

Full diagnosis path

Pull the audit trail for the call, then read the agent's granted tool scope: was the tool in its roster, and who approved that grant? Fix the grant (least privilege across agents and MCP), add a regression that proves the call is now refused, and only then consider whether prompt injection steered the attempt — content-level defenses come second to authority-level ones.

Practiced in Week 9 (least privilege, audit evidence, red team)
“Staging and production answer the same question differently.”

Same prompt, same document set, different behavior per environment.

Full diagnosis path

Diff the two environments where they are defined: the IaC plan and drift report. Model version pins, retrieval parameters, guardrail policy versions, and corpus snapshots are all configuration. If drift exists, the fix travels through the CI/CD gates like any other change — hand-patching production recreates the problem with interest.

Practiced in Week 7 (drift, plan discipline) with Week 1's model-selection pins
“Answer quality drops only during traffic spikes, then recovers.”

Off-peak answers are excellent. Peak-hour answers are thin.

Full diagnosis path

Correlate quality scores with saturation: at peak, is context assembly truncating evidence to fit the token budget, or is token-aware admission deferring retrieval work? Both are designed degradation; the question is whether the degradation is the one you chose. If assembly is clean, then check provider throttling and queue depth on the event backbone.

Practiced in Week 6 (token-aware backpressure), Week 5 (budgeted assembly), Week 8 (saturation SLIs)

Trade-off calls — commit, then stress it

Each call has a defensible default in this system. Pick one, check it, then read what would change your mind. That second half is the part clients pay for. 0 of 4 worked through

“Which customers are affected if the payments service degrades?”

Pick the retrieval plane you would route this question to.

What would change your mind

If the question were “find past incidents that looked like this one,” similarity wins: there is no explicit relationship to traverse, only resemblance. The serving path keeps both planes because real client questions alternate between the two shapes, and context assembly can merge their evidence.

Practiced in Week 5 (retriever choice per question)
“Ten thousand documents need re-processing after a policy change.”

Pick how the work enters the system.

What would change your mind

A user waiting on one answer right now needs the synchronous path; queueing an interactive request buys latency and nothing else. The split is the one both diagrams draw: interactive work on the serving path, bulk and failure-tolerant work through the backbone.

Practiced in Week 6 (event judgement, delivery semantics)
“The eval gate failed on a release the client is waiting for.”

Pick the default response.

What would change your mind

If the failing eval is known-flaky, the regression is bounded and user-invisible, and rollback is rehearsed, then a human can overrule the gate, in writing, through the audit trail. That is why the gates carry human approval: the default is hold, and the override is owned, not silent.

Practiced in Week 7 (gates, approval) and Week 10 (reversible rollout)
“A message keeps failing processing. Retry forever, or divert?”

Pick the failure policy for the pipeline.

What would change your mind

Nothing removes the bound, but the bound's size is a judgement call. A transient infrastructure error with an idempotent handler earns more retries; a deterministic parse failure earns one. The invariant the diagram draws is that diverted work is retained and owned, never dropped.

Practiced in Week 6 (delivery recovery, idempotent side effects)
